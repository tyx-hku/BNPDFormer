\begin{abstract}
Dynamic bottleneck prediction is essential in multi-entity production systems.
Existing studies face limitations in observation scope and bottleneck supervision.
They mainly rely on single-entity data from workstations, with limited representation of multiple entities including workers, materials, buffers, and logistics and the relations among them.
This narrow observation scope leads to incomplete factory-graph modelling, making it difficult to capture delayed propagation across resources and thereby reducing prediction accuracy.
In addition, constructing reliable bottleneck supervision is difficult: bottleneck events are scarce, while simple threshold-based labels may mistake short-term changes for lasting bottlenecks and provide little information about their causes.
To address these limitations, we propose the \textbf{Process-Informed Heterogeneous-Graph Spatiotemporal Transformer with Delay-aware Modeling (PHGST-DM)}.
Firstly, to broaden the observation scope, PHGST-DM combines a heterogeneous process--logistics graph with delay-aware modeling guided by recurrent operating patterns, linking earlier resource states to operating changes that emerge elsewhere after a propagation delay.
Secondly, to improve bottleneck supervision, process-informed supervision generates labels for sustained bottlenecks and their causes, while clustering identifies repeated operating patterns.
In addition, the model extends bottleneck prediction beyond simple temporal estimates to provide a more comprehensive bottleneck description.
It jointly outputs bottleneck station, onset, duration, process cause, and remaining order time.
Experiments in the evaluated simulation setting demonstrate higher event-prediction accuracy than the compared baselines and detailed bottleneck forecasts, while ablation studies support the contributions of heterogeneous resource information, process-informed supervision, and delay-aware modeling.
These results support advance warning and joint bottleneck forecasting in the evaluated multi-entity production setting.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction} \label{sec:intro}
%% ========================================================================

A production bottleneck is the resource or process that limits system throughput at a given time~\cite{subramaniyan2023throughput,tang2024comprehensive}. Its identity and location are dynamic: changes in resource availability, material supply, work-in-process, and operating conditions can cause the bottleneck to migrate during production~\cite{lai2021data}. Timely and accurate prediction of these changes is therefore important for production management: advance warning can inform resource allocation and production scheduling.

Existing bottleneck-prediction studies face two limitations. First, existing studies mainly model workstation data. Time-series and recurrent methods forecast active periods, blockage, starvation, or bottleneck scores from workstation-centred observations~\cite{li2011throughput,subramaniyan2018data,lai2018twolayer,liu2023lstmdam}; graph-based methods further introduce task or material-flow relations~\cite{lai2023bstan,yin2025btgcn,ma2023tusb,cen2025dstnn,wu2025knowledge}. Nevertheless, workers, material availability, buffers, and transport resources are rarely represented together, even though their interactions can determine where and when a bottleneck appears. This narrow observation scope leaves the factory graph incomplete and makes delayed cross-resource propagation difficult to capture: an operating change may pass through transport and buffering before emerging as blockage or starvation at a downstream station, so the resource where the operating change originates may differ from the resource that later constrains throughput~\cite{gu2015passive,zhang2022vulnerability}. Recurrent transmission and local graph aggregation can capture temporal or neighbourhood dependence~\cite{liu2023lstmdam,yin2025btgcn}, but do not explicitly align an earlier cross-resource pattern with its delayed consequence.

Second, bottleneck supervision is difficult to construct. Bottleneck events are scarce in normal operation, and deliberately inducing failures, worker absence, material shortages, or logistics interruption to obtain training data is not feasible during real factory production. Conventional scalar scores and turning-point indicators derived from utilization, blockage, or starvation~\cite{subramaniyan2023throughput,lai2021data,su2022dynamic} can support retrospective analysis. However, when such indicators are used alone to construct prediction targets, they reduce a changing production process to a narrow decision criterion and may ignore the combined evidence of queues, resource availability, material shortages, and logistics conditions. Without this process context, the resulting labels may mistake short-term changes for lasting bottlenecks and assign similar labels to events with different operational causes. Such ambiguous targets can limit prediction accuracy and make the causes of predicted events difficult to interpret. These limitations motivate a unified approach to heterogeneous observation, process-informed supervision, and delay-aware prediction.

To address these limitations, we propose the \textbf{Process-Informed Heterogeneous-Graph Spatiotemporal Transformer with Delay-aware Modeling (PHGST-DM)}. First, to broaden the observation scope and capture delayed interactions, PHGST-DM combines a heterogeneous process--logistics graph with delay-aware modeling guided by recurrent operating patterns. An open-workspace factory model developed for NVIDIA Isaac Sim provides traceable trajectories of workstations, workers, material supply, buffers, and transport resources under configurable operating conditions~\cite{lu2020digital,isaacsim2026,xue2026safe}. These observations are organized as a heterogeneous process--logistics graph: workstations and handling resources are represented as interacting entities, while material shortage, buffer occupancy, labour availability, and transport delay provide resource-specific operational context. This representation brings production and logistics observations into a shared graph, allowing the predictor to account for interactions beyond workstation states alone. Delay-aware modeling matches recent resource trajectories to fixed temporal-pattern prototypes and conditions cross-resource attention on the resulting pattern context. Earlier resource states can therefore contribute to the prediction of bottlenecks that emerge elsewhere after transport or buffering delay. Second, to improve bottleneck supervision, process-informed supervision combines queue, blockage, starvation, availability, shortage, and logistics evidence with persistence requirements. This supervision distinguishes sustained bottleneck events from short-term fluctuations and assigns observable process-cause targets, connecting supervision to traceable production evidence. Clustering summarizes training-only short temporal patches as recurrent operating-pattern prototypes, providing the pattern context used for delay-aware modeling. Together, these components provide bottleneck-event and process-cause supervision.

In addition, PHGST-DM jointly outputs bottleneck station, onset, duration, observable process cause, and remaining order time. In the evaluated simulation setting, it achieves higher event-prediction accuracy than the compared baselines. Ablation studies assess the roles of heterogeneous resource information, process-informed supervision, and delay-aware modeling in these forecasts.

The contributions of this work are summarized as follows.

(1) We extend dynamic bottleneck prediction from workstation-centred observations to heterogeneous multi-entity factory data. Workstations, workers, material-supply states, buffers, and transport resources are jointly represented with their process and logistics relations, enabling the model to capture bottlenecks arising from production, labour, material, and handling interactions rather than from workstation states alone.

(2) We develop \emph{process-informed supervision and temporal pattern clustering} for bottleneck data whose events are scarce and weakly separable from normal fluctuations. Resource-specific rules and persistence constraints construct operationally meaningful event and process-cause targets, deterministic cause-aligned regime identifiers provide structured context, and training-only clustering summarizes short temporal patches as recurrent operating-pattern prototypes. This design provides reliable supervision to improve bottleneck-identification accuracy.

(3) We develop \emph{recurrent-pattern-guided delay-aware modeling for heterogeneous production resources}. Recent queue trajectories are matched to activity-pattern prototypes, and the resulting context conditions attention across the process--logistics graph. This design uses historical resource interactions to predict emerging bottlenecks, with component studies evaluating its contribution to distant-onset warning.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:review} reviews bottleneck detection and prediction methods and spatiotemporal graph forecasting. Section~\ref{sec:problem} formulates the station-level dynamic bottleneck prediction problem. Section~\ref{sec:method} presents the factory-model data pipeline and PHGST-DM architecture. Section~\ref{sec:exp} reports the experimental setup and results.

\section{Literature review} \label{sec:review}
%% ========================================================================

\subsection{Dynamic bottleneck prediction concepts and task scope}

A throughput bottleneck constrains system output; high utilization, failure, or a local queue alone does not establish that role. Bottleneck-identification methods must therefore be interpreted against production relations and available observations~\cite{subramaniyan2023throughput}. The constrained resource can change with resource availability, material supply, and operating conditions. Lai et al.~\cite{lai2021data} combined production data with factory-physics knowledge to identify shifting bottlenecks in non-serial systems. Detection describes an observed constraint, whereas prediction infers a future one. The distinction between identification, diagnosis, prediction, and prescription~\cite{subramaniyan2021ai} is relevant here: updating detection in real time does not itself provide advance warning.





Existing prediction studies follow three main approaches: extrapolating bottleneck indicators, adapting to changing production conditions, and explicitly modelling production relations. Subramaniyan et al.~\cite{subramaniyan2018data} combined ARIMA with machine active-period analysis to predict throughput bottlenecks, while Huang et al.~\cite{huang2019proactive} used future bottleneck predictions to support proactive task dispatching. Liu et al.~\cite{liu2023lstmdam} constructed bottleneck indicators from station supply--demand relations, learned temporal dependencies with a dual-attention LSTM, and updated the model through concept-drift detection and transfer learning. Ma et al.~\cite{ma2023tusb} jointly modelled spatial and temporal information for high-variety shops with complex routings, and Lai et al.~\cite{lai2023bstan} predicted blockage and starvation from station relations before inferring future bottlenecks. These studies extend prediction from single-indicator extrapolation to production relations and adaptation to changing operating conditions.

Event-based prediction gives this warning an explicit resource and time interval: whether a bottleneck will occur, where it will occur, when it will start, and how long it will persist. For an already active event, the relevant temporal target is its remaining duration. These distinctions connect the predicted indicators to the event-level outputs considered in this study.

\subsection{Bottleneck indicators and input representation}

Constructing a state representation from production records requires explicit definitions of the observed resources, time range, and indicator semantics. Queue length, waiting time, activity ratio, and blockage or starvation time describe different aspects of resource operation and can form a multi-dimensional observation. Skoogh et al.~\cite{subramaniyan2023throughput} group bottleneck-detection information into queue states, process states, and combinations of the two, representing product accumulation and waiting, resource activity, and constrained operating conditions. Subramaniyan et al.~\cite{subramaniyan2016realtime} computed average active periods from machine states and their timestamps in real-time MES data to identify production bottlenecks. Compared with a point-wise state or a window-level activity ratio, a continuous active period preserves persistence information, showing that indicator design must also reflect the temporal organization of production changes.

The resulting multi-dimensional indicators can be processed in two complementary ways: process knowledge can define state rules, or data similarity can reveal operating regimes. The first approach combines resource activity, material supply, and neighbouring-process relations to interpret blockage, starvation, or other production conditions. Lai et al.~\cite{lai2021data} combined blockage and starvation information with production-structure knowledge for complex manufacturing systems, while Liu et al.~\cite{liu2023lstmdam} used station supply--demand relations to construct predictive indicators. The second approach clusters machine indicator sequences or multi-dimensional window observations according to similarity. Subramaniyan et al.~\cite{subramaniyan2020clustering} generated bottleneck-indicator time series from event logs and used dynamic-time-warping distance with agglomerative hierarchical clustering to group machines with similar production dynamics. For multi-dimensional window samples, K-means can also summarize similar operating conditions, although its result depends on the feature representation and similarity measure~\cite{jain2010clustering}. Process rules can define supervision, while clustering summarizes recurrent patterns for representation learning. Their combination need not convert cluster assignments into bottleneck or cause labels.

Combining operational indicators with resource types and rule-derived states produces inputs with both continuous and categorical features. Separate projections and embeddings can preserve these differences before the features are combined, as illustrated by Temporal Fusion Transformer~\cite{lim2021tft}. In manufacturing, these representations must also preserve the resource and process meanings of each observation.

\subsection{Delayed cross-resource effects in production systems}\label{sec:manufacturing-delay}

Manufacturing-system studies show that finite buffers can delay the transmission of disruption effects between resources. Finite buffers can temporarily decouple neighbouring stations: an upstream interruption need not starve a downstream machine until the available work-in-process is consumed, while a downstream interruption need not block an upstream machine until the intervening storage fills. Gu et al.~\cite{gu2013hidden} analysed these buffering effects through maintenance opportunity windows in discrete and complex production lines, with simulations and real automotive-assembly-plant cases. Gu et al.~\cite{gu2015passive} further predicted failure-induced starvation or blockage intervals on bottleneck machines when other machines fail upstream or downstream, and validated their algorithm using simulations and an automotive assembly plant. These studies establish an operational reason to consider both the originating disruption and its later effect on a different resource.

Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2022vulnerability} made the delay explicit in a finite-buffer smartphone-assembly model by defining \emph{Bottleneck Time Delay} as the interval from a disruption to production stoppage at the bottleneck station. Their analysis relates this interval to the disruption location and the intervening buffer occupancy, and distinguishes it from the delay before the end-of-line station stops. The study provides analytical and numerical evidence through a smartphone-production digital-twin evaluation tool. Taken together, this literature supports delayed transmission of disruption effects, rather than the assumption that every local disruption immediately changes system throughput. It also distinguishes such transmission from bottleneck migration: a bottleneck can be affected by a remote disruption without its location changing. For prediction, this motivates using earlier observations of related resources together with the target resource's recent state. The modelling challenge is therefore to use this lagged cross-resource evidence for event prediction in heterogeneous, multi-entity production systems.

\subsection{Graph-based spatiotemporal modelling and event prediction}

Process precedence, material transfer, and shared tasks connect manufacturing resources into networks. Graph neural networks aggregate related-node information~\cite{wu2021gnn}, using normalized connectivity in graph convolutional networks (GCN)~\cite{kipf2017gcn} or learned neighbour weights in graph attention networks (GAT)~\cite{velickovic2018gat}. Dynamic forecasting additionally requires temporal organization of those interactions.

Traffic forecasting offers methodological references for this organization: DCRNN combines diffusion with recurrence~\cite{li2018dcrnn}, STGCN combines graph and temporal convolution~\cite{yu2018stgcn}, and Graph WaveNet and STSGCN extend learned connectivity and joint spatiotemporal interaction~\cite{wu2019graphwavenet,song2020stsgcn}. These mechanisms can organize multi-node histories, but road connectivity does not directly specify manufacturing process, logistics, or resource-coordination relations.

Manufacturing graph models differ in the production objects and prediction targets they represent. BSTAN uses stations as nodes and production-line relations as edges, combining GAT with a gated recurrent unit (GRU) to predict blockage and starvation before inferring future bottlenecks~\cite{lai2023bstan}. BTGCN represents ERP-managed tasks in a shipbuilding enterprise as nodes and links them through shared operators~\cite{yin2025btgcn}. Its GCN and GRU predict task-execution frequency for bottleneck identification under a minimum-throughput criterion. Dual-BDSTN combines GRU and self-attention for temporal features with a dynamic graph for material-flow-related spatial dependence~\cite{cen2025dstnn}. Cross-attention integrates these representations to predict blockage and starvation time. Because the nodes, edges, and outputs have different production meanings, comparisons must specify both the analysis object and the prediction target.

These manufacturing models organize information through production relations; the remaining question is how to use historical evidence across those relations. Local aggregation requires multiple propagation steps to connect distant resources, while adaptive relations extend connectivity but still require manufacturing-specific interpretation. In coupled production and logistics, related-resource histories may carry warning evidence before the target resource visibly becomes constrained. This motivates considering attention-based organization of historical cross-resource information.

Spatiotemporal attention offers another way to connect historical positions and distant resources. Multi-head self-attention models dependencies across representation subspaces~\cite{vaswani2017attention}, with input, position, and output design adapted to forecasting~\cite{wen2023transformers}. GMAN combines spatial and temporal attention with historical-to-future transformation~\cite{zheng2020gman}. PDFormer uses geographic and semantic masks, short-term historical patterns, and propagation-delay-aware feature transformation for traffic prediction~\cite{jiang2023pdformer}. These are relevant design foundations for PHGST-DM, whose observations and relations must instead express heterogeneous production and logistics conditions. Predictive attention associations do not establish physical propagation paths.

The representation produced by a spatiotemporal model must also match the prediction target. Continuous-indicator prediction concerns future values, whereas event prediction involves categories, onset time, and duration. Tax et al.~\cite{tax2017predictive} used a long short-term memory network (LSTM) to predict the next activity and its occurrence time from process logs and to estimate the remaining completion time. Jin et al.~\cite{jin2023stgnpp} proposed STGNPP, which combines traffic states, road relations, and congestion-event history with spatiotemporal representations, a continuous-time GRU, and a point-process intensity function to predict the occurrence time and duration of the next congestion event. These studies provide different implementations for joint prediction of events and temporal attributes and show that output design should follow the definition of the target event.



\subsection{Research gaps}

Three gaps motivate this study. First, representative manufacturing graph predictors mainly organize machines, stations, or tasks. Joint representation of workers, handling devices, buffers, and material-supply evidence with their process--logistics relations remains limited.

Second, process-consistent event supervision remains difficult. Isolated indicator thresholds can confuse transient variation with sustained constraints and provide limited cause information. Event and cause targets therefore need definitions traceable to observable production states.

Third, manufacturing studies support delayed cross-resource effects~\cite{gu2013hidden,gu2015passive,zhang2022vulnerability}, but a heterogeneous predictor must organize the relevant historical observations for future station, onset, and duration prediction. These gaps motivate the multi-entity representation, process-informed supervision, and recurrent-pattern-guided delay-aware modeling developed below.

\subsection{Production system and resource relations}

The system comprises interacting resource entities represented by a heterogeneous process--logistics graph. We distinguish event-supervised targets from contextual resources whose states influence production constraints.

\subsubsection{Production task and resource entities}

We consider a production system coupling processing and assembly with storage, handling, transport, and worker service. A workstation denotes a processing or assembly unit, including automated equipment and manually operated workbenches. Bottleneck prediction observes the controlled production process; it does not replace its admission or allocation controller. The evaluated water-pipe line and collection conditions are described in Section~\ref{sec:exp}.

We denote the complete resource set by $\mathcal{R}$ and its workstation, gantry, transport-robot, worker, and buffer subsets by superscripts $m$, $g$, $a$, $h$, and $b$, respectively:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}^{m}\cup\mathcal{R}^{g}\cup
\mathcal{R}^{a}\cup\mathcal{R}^{h}\cup\mathcal{R}^{b}.
\end{equation}
A transport robot is an automated guided vehicle (AGV). The evaluated graph contains 38 nodes: 7 workstations, 4 gantries, 4 transport robots, 5 workers, and 18 buffers. Material supply enters through resource-associated shortage, inbound-waiting, and buffer features rather than separate material nodes. Bottleneck-event targets are assigned to the subset $\mathcal{S}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{R}^{m}\cup\mathcal{R}^{g}\cup\mathcal{R}^{a}.
\end{equation}
Worker and buffer nodes remain in the observation graph because labour availability and storage occupancy influence other resources, but the current implementation does not assign them station-event targets.

\subsubsection{Heterogeneous process--logistics graph}

Using $E$ for the edge set and $A$ for the weighted adjacency matrix, we represent the production system as a weighted undirected graph:
\begin{equation}
G=(\mathcal{R},E,A),\qquad
E=E^{p}\cup E^{f}\cup E^{b}\cup E^{l}\cup E^{w}.
\end{equation}
The relation subsets $E^{p}$, $E^{f}$, $E^{b}$, $E^{l}$, and $E^{w}$ encode process chains, parallel workstations of the same equipment family, buffer--workstation associations, handling--processing links, and same-type handling links, respectively. Edge weights and attention-mask construction are specified in Section~\ref{sec:method}.

These relations organize process dependency, storage-mediated constraints, and logistics interaction. Geographic proximity and behavioural similarity are treated separately by the encoder; graph edges denote possible interactions rather than verified physical causal paths.

\subsection{State representation and prediction targets}

Each prediction sample pairs a resource history with future event, occupancy, process-cause, and order-progress targets.

Table~\ref{tab:feature-groups} summarizes their semantics. Labour saturation is derived from worker states and supplied to workstations. The offline bottleneck-score column is excluded from the input to avoid reproducing a supervision signal.

Let $t$ denote the forecast origin, $\tau$ a historical window, and $n$ one of $N$ resources. Collecting their states $x_{\tau,n}$ over $T_{\mathrm{in}}=30$ historical windows gives the input tensor
\begin{equation}
X_t=\left[x_{\tau,n}\right]_
{\substack{\tau=t-T_{\mathrm{in}},\ldots,t-1\\n=1,\ldots,N}}
\in\mathbb{R}^{T_{\mathrm{in}}\times N\times 27}.
\end{equation}
The future label grid contains $H_{\mathrm{label}}=30$ one-minute windows. A new bottleneck is eligible only if it starts within a specified time after the forecast origin. We call this maximum offset the \emph{onset limit}, denoted by $H_{\mathrm{start}}$. For example, Onset limit~$\leq5$ admits a new onset at most 5~min ahead while retaining the full 30-min label grid. Experiments compare limits of 5, 10, and 15~min.

\subsubsection{Process-informed supervision for bottleneck events}

A bottleneck label must distinguish a production-relevant constraint from ordinary activity or travel. For resource $n$ of type $\rho(n)$, the operational rule $\Phi_{\rho(n)}$ maps the resource state and observed history to a raw binary indicator $b^{\mathrm{raw}}_{t,n}$:
\begin{equation}
b^{\mathrm{raw}}_{t,n}=\Phi_{\rho(n)}(x_{t,n},X_t)\in\{0,1\}.
\end{equation}
The rule combines operational evidence rather than treating an injected operating variation or ordinary resource activity as a bottleneck. Its resource-specific implementation is given in Algorithm~\ref{alg:targets} and Appendix~\ref{app:additional}.

To construct sustained occupancy $o$, short-run removal $\mathcal{D}_{m}$ filters the raw binary grid using a minimum run length $m$. Gap filling $\mathcal{F}_{g}$ then joins retained regions separated by an internal gap of at most $g=1$ window. For each resource $n$, with the colon denoting its full episode sequence, these operations give
\begin{equation}
o_{:,n}=\mathcal{F}_{g}\!\left(\mathcal{D}_{m}
\left(b^{\mathrm{raw}}_{:,n}\right)\right).
\end{equation}
The occupancy grid $o$ is an offline supervision target. At forecast origin $t$, the history-only state $h_{t-1,n}$ is computed from observations strictly before $t$, including any persistence or gap checks. It can differ from the retrospective label $o_{t-1,n}$ because the latter can use later observations. Inference therefore receives the observed-prefix state, not the offline occupancy label.

For a history ending immediately before window $t$, let $w_{t,n}$ indicate an eligible event on resource $n$, with onset offset $s_{t,n}$ and future length $d_{t,n}$ measured in one-minute windows. Its event target is
\begin{equation}
y_{t,n}^{\mathrm{evt}}=(w_{t,n},s_{t,n},d_{t,n}).
\end{equation}
If the history-only state $h_{t-1,n}=1$ and the future run begins at offset zero, the event is \emph{ongoing}, and the duration target describes its future remainder. A run with $s_{t,n}>0$ is \emph{upcoming}. If several runs are available for one station, the longest eligible run is selected rather than the earliest run.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:targets} in Section~\ref{sec:method} specifies construction of these targets and the associated regime context.


\subsubsection{Cause and order-level targets}

The window-level process-cause target $c_t$ describes observed conditions accompanying bottleneck evidence. Its fixed priority is material shortage, transport delay, queue buildup, and downstream blockage, inferred from operational variables rather than the injected variation dimension. These categories describe process symptoms, not independently verified root causes.

The graph-level cause head uses the four-class set $\mathcal{C}$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{C}=\{&
\text{material shortage},\text{transport delay},\\
&\text{queue buildup},\text{downstream blockage}\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Cause targets differ from deterministic regime identifiers, which condition the event representation; Algorithm~\ref{alg:targets} specifies both.

The order-level target $\ell_t$ is the number of future one-minute windows until all jobs are complete under the configured prediction limit. It estimates order progress alongside the station-level event report.

\subsection{Multi-task prediction formulation}

With learned parameters $\theta$, the predictor $f_{\theta}$ maps the history and graph to event reports for all supervised resources and graph-level estimates of cause and remaining order time:
\begin{equation}
f_{\theta}:(X_t,G)\longmapsto
\left(
\{\hat w_{t,n},\hat s_{t,n},\hat d_{t,n}\}_{n\in\mathcal{S}},
\hat c_t,\hat\ell_t
\right).
\end{equation}
Hats distinguish predictions from the targets defined above: $\hat w_{t,n}$, $\hat s_{t,n}$, and $\hat d_{t,n}$ describe event occurrence, onset, and future duration; $\hat c_t$ and $\hat\ell_t$ describe process cause and remaining order time. Earlier states of related resources can provide evidence for a future event; PHGST-DM uses this history without introducing a physical propagation-delay target. Evaluation and report-matching criteria are specified in Section~\ref{sec:exp}.

\subsection{Method overview}

PHGST-DM integrates heterogeneous process--logistics observations, process-informed event and cause supervision, and recurrent-pattern-conditioned attention for joint bottleneck prediction. The manufacturing-specific design connects multi-entity resource states and sustained-event supervision to forecasts of bottleneck location, timing, process context, and order progress. The pipeline is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\text{factory model}&\rightarrow\text{window/event construction}\\
&\rightarrow\text{factory graph}\rightarrow\text{joint prediction}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}




Figure~\ref{fig:model-architecture} shows how grouped resource features, temporal and structural embeddings, and a fixed pattern bank feed the spatiotemporal encoder. Station-level and graph-level heads decode the joint outputs defined in Section~\ref{sec:problem}.

\subsection{Data preparation and graph instantiation}

The following steps instantiate the observations, targets, and graph defined in Section~\ref{sec:problem}.

\subsubsection{Open-workspace factory model}

The NVIDIA Isaac Sim factory model records resource transitions, job traces, buffer events, transport tasks, and material inventory at logical-second resolution. Streams are synchronized by episode and time. Episodes affected by the known simulator deadlock defect are discarded as invalid records; valid episodes end after all ten products complete.

Configurable resource capacity, service behaviour, and temporary operating variations generate trajectories under different conditions. Section~\ref{sec:exp} specifies the four variation dimensions, intensity levels, and collection protocol.

\subsubsection{Multi-entity aggregation and target instantiation}

Resource-state intervals are reconstructed from second-resolution logs and aligned into non-overlapping one-minute windows. Processing, blockage, starvation, and stoppage durations are accumulated over their overlap with each window; active and unavailable ratios divide these durations by window length. Queue features count waiting jobs and summarize waiting times. Buffer occupancy is averaged within the window, carrying forward the preceding value when no update is recorded; growth is the difference between window-end and window-start counts. Process and transport relations supply upstream/downstream state ratios, route delay, inbound waiting, and shortage propagation.

Windows share a fixed resource ordering. Missing numeric entries are filled with zero, type indicators are appended, and labour saturation is recomputed from worker activity. Histories and future grids follow Section~\ref{sec:problem}, with a one-window stride and no crossing of episode boundaries. Standardization is fitted on training histories and reused for validation and testing.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:targets} derives event, occupancy, prefix, cause, regime-context, and remaining-time supervision from operational states, excluding the exported bottleneck score.

Rule-derived regime identifiers provide context for event representations and contrastive learning, while training-only clustering constructs the temporal prototype bank from recurrent histories.

\begin{algorithm*}[t]

  }

$(w_{t,n},s_{t,n},d_{t,n})\leftarrow(0,0,0)$\;

\end{algorithm*}

\subsubsection{Heterogeneous factory graph and attention masks}

The weighted adjacency $A$ follows the relations in Section~\ref{sec:problem}. The five relation groups use weights $1.0$, $0.8$, $0.6$, $0.5$, and $0.3$, respectively; self-loops have weight $1.0$. Floyd--Warshall computation gives the shortest-path hop distance $D^{\mathrm{hop}}_{ij}$ between resources $i$ and $j$. Geographic attention uses a hop cutoff $\delta_G=4$ through the additive mask
\begin{equation}
M^G_{ij}=
\begin{cases}
0, & D^{\mathrm{hop}}_{ij}<\delta_G,\\
-\infty, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad \delta_G=4.
\end{equation}
A zero mask entry permits attention, whereas $-\infty$ blocks it. Geographic attention therefore connects resources fewer than four hops apart.

Semantic attention instead links similar training trajectories. Let $\bar x_{:,i}$ denote the channel-averaged training trajectory of resource $i$. Its semantic distance from resource $j$ is measured by one minus their Pearson correlation:
\begin{equation}
D^{\mathrm{sem}}_{ij}
=1-\mathrm{corr}(\bar{x}_{:,i},\bar{x}_{:,j}).
\end{equation}
The five nearest trajectories are unmasked in the semantic attention mask $M^S$. Pearson correlation replaces traffic PDFormer's dynamic-time-warping distance in this short-episode setting. Eight nontrivial normalized-Laplacian eigenvectors supply structural positions. Algorithm~\ref{alg:graph-masks} summarizes construction; all trajectory-derived quantities use training data only.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Factory graph and attention-mask construction.}
\label{alg:graph-masks}
\KwIn{resources $\mathcal{V}$; typed relations $\mathcal{R}$; scaled training histories $X^{\mathrm{tr}}$; $\delta_G{=}4$; $k_S{=}5$; $d_{\mathrm{lap}}{=}8$}
\KwOut{weighted adjacency $A$; geographic mask $M^G$; semantic mask $M^S$; structural positions $E_{\mathrm{lap}}$}
$A\leftarrow I_{|\mathcal{V}|}$\;
\ForEach{typed relation $(i,j,w)\in\mathcal{R}$}{
$A_{ij}\leftarrow A_{ji}\leftarrow\max(A_{ij},w)$\;
}
$D^{\mathrm{hop}}\leftarrow\mathrm{FloydWarshall}(\mathbf{1}[A>0])$\;
\ForEach{$(i,j)\in\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}$}{
$M^G_{ij}\leftarrow0$ if $D^{\mathrm{hop}}_{ij}<\delta_G$, otherwise $-\infty$\;
}
$\bar x_{:,i}\leftarrow\mathrm{MeanChannels}(X^{\mathrm{tr}}_{:,:,i,:})$ for every $i$\;
$D^{\mathrm{sem}}_{ij}\leftarrow1-\mathrm{corr}(\bar x_{:,i},\bar x_{:,j})$\;
\ForEach{$i\in\mathcal{V}$}{
$\mathcal{N}^{S}_i\leftarrow$ indices of the $k_S$ smallest finite values in $D^{\mathrm{sem}}_{i,:}$\;
$M^S_{ij}\leftarrow0$ for $j\in\mathcal{N}^{S}_i\cup\{i\}$, and $-\infty$ otherwise\;
}
$L\leftarrow I-D_A^{-1/2}AD_A^{-1/2}$\;
$E_{\mathrm{lap}}\leftarrow$ first $d_{\mathrm{lap}}$ nontrivial eigenvectors of $L$\;
\Return $A,M^G,M^S,E_{\mathrm{lap}}$\;
\end{algorithm}

In Algorithm~\ref{alg:graph-masks}, $\mathcal{V}$ denotes resource nodes and $\mathcal{R}$ the list of typed relations $(i,j,w)$ with edge weight $w$. The matrix $D_A$ contains adjacency row sums on its diagonal, $I$ is the identity matrix, and $L$ is the normalized graph Laplacian. The set $\mathcal{N}^{S}_i$ contains the $k_S$ semantic neighbours of resource $i$, and $d_{\mathrm{lap}}$ is the number of retained structural eigenvectors.

\subsection{Delay-aware heterogeneous spatiotemporal encoding}



\subsubsection{Grouped heterogeneous state embedding}

The 27 input channels are partitioned into six groups: queue, stall, logistics, shortage, context, and type. For group $q$, the feature vector $x^{(q)}_{t,n}$ is projected with a learned matrix $W_q$ and bias $b_q$ to obtain the embedding $e^{(q)}_{t,n}$:
\begin{equation}
e^{(q)}_{t,n}=\mathrm{GELU}
\left(W_q x^{(q)}_{t,n}+b_q\right).
\end{equation}
GELU denotes the Gaussian error linear unit. Mean pooling the six group embeddings gives the combined resource embedding $e_{t,n}$:
\begin{equation}
e_{t,n}=\frac{1}{6}\sum_{q=1}^{6}e^{(q)}_{t,n}.
\end{equation}
Mean pooling gives each semantic group equal weight regardless of channel count.

The encoder input $H^{(0)}$ combines the grouped embeddings $E_{\mathrm{group}}(X_t)$ with sinusoidal temporal positions $E_{\mathrm{time}}$, projected graph eigenvectors $E_{\mathrm{lap}}(G)$, and learned resource identities $E_{\mathrm{node}}$:
\begin{equation}
H^{(0)}=E_{\mathrm{group}}(X_t)+
E_{\mathrm{time}}+E_{\mathrm{lap}}(G)+E_{\mathrm{node}}.
\end{equation}
Day-of-week and time-of-day embeddings are disabled because an episode is driven by order progress and operating variations rather than calendar periodicity.

\subsubsection{Delay-aware spatiotemporal modeling}

Each encoder block uses temporal, geographic, and semantic attention~\cite{jiang2023pdformer}, organized around resource histories, process--logistics relations, and behavioural similarity. For branch $b\in\{T,G,S\}$, respectively, scaled dot-product attention is computed as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Attn}_{b}(Q_b,K_b,V_b;M_b)
=\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(
\frac{Q_bK_b^\top}{\sqrt{d_h}}+M_b
\right)V_b ,
\end{equation}
where $Q_b$, $K_b$, and $V_b$ are query, key, and value matrices, $d_h$ is the query/key dimension per head, and $M_b$ is the additive mask. The superscript $\top$ denotes transpose; softmax normalizes scores over attended positions. Temporal attention operates within each resource history without a cross-node mask, while geographic and semantic attention use $M^G$ and $M^S$.

Training-only k-means fits sixteen prototypes from three-window sequences of resource activity ratios; online queries use three-window sequences of queue length. Both variables use the training-fitted feature scaling described above. Let $P_{t,n}$ denote a recent queue-length patch and $K^P$ the fixed activity-prototype bank. Matching them produces the context $C^P_{t,n}$:
\begin{equation}
C^P_{t,n}=
\mathrm{softmax}\!\left(
\frac{f_Q^P(P_{t,n})f_K^P(K^P)^\top}{\sqrt{d_h}}
\right)f_V^P(K^P).
\end{equation}
The learned functions $f_Q^P(\cdot)$, $f_K^P(\cdot)$, and $f_V^P(\cdot)$ project queries, keys, and values, respectively. The matched context is added to the geographic key, conditioning cross-resource interaction on recurrent activity patterns and current queue evolution. Pattern matching operates directly on historical patches, without a Fourier transform or an explicit physical propagation-time target. Algorithm~\ref{alg:pattern-bank} specifies prototype fitting.

At encoder block $l$, the preceding representation $H^{(l-1)}$ feeds three branches. Their temporal, geographic, and semantic outputs are denoted by $H_T^{(l)}$, $H_G^{(l)}$, and $H_S^{(l)}$; the geographic branch also receives the pattern context $C^P$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
H_T^{(l)}&=\mathrm{Attn}_T(H^{(l-1)}),\\
H_G^{(l)}&=\mathrm{Attn}_G(H^{(l-1)},M^G,C^P),\\
H_S^{(l)}&=\mathrm{Attn}_S(H^{(l-1)},M^S).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Branch outputs are concatenated and projected, followed by pre-normalized residual and feed-forward layers; FFN in Algorithm~\ref{alg:phgst-predict} denotes the feed-forward network. Five blocks use latent dimension 80 and $4/2/2$ temporal/geographic/semantic heads.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Training-only temporal pattern clustering.}
\label{alg:pattern-bank}
\KwIn{scaled training histories $X^{\mathrm{tr}}$; activity channel $c_{\mathrm{act}}$; patch length $S{=}3$; centres $K{=}16$; iterations $J{=}10$; seed $0$}
\KwOut{pattern bank $K^P=\{k^P_1,\ldots,k^P_K\}$}
$\mathcal{P}\leftarrow\varnothing$\;
\ForEach{training history $X_b\in X^{\mathrm{tr}}$}{
  \ForEach{resource $n$ and valid start $u$}{
append $X_{b,u:u+S-1,n,c_{\mathrm{act}}}$ to $\mathcal{P}$\;
  }
}
initialize $K^P$ with $K$ distinct patches sampled from $\mathcal{P}$ using seed $0$\;
\For{$j=1,\ldots,J$}{
assign each $p\in\mathcal{P}$ to $a(p)=\arg\min_k\lVert p-k^P_k\rVert_2^2$\;
  \ForEach{centre $k$ with nonempty assignment $\mathcal{P}_k$}{
$k^P_k\leftarrow |\mathcal{P}_k|^{-1}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}_k}p$\;
  }
}
\Return $K^P$ reshaped to $K\times S\times1$\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Multi-task bottleneck prediction and optimization}

The encoder feeds station-level event and occupancy heads and graph-level cause and remaining-time heads, optimized jointly and decoded under validation-selected thresholds.

\subsubsection{Event, occupancy, and explanatory heads}

Let $z_{t,n}$ be the final representation of supervised station $n$. Learned scalar-output heads $f_{\mathrm{cont}}$ and $f_{\mathrm{onset}}$ predict the probabilities of event continuation $p^{\mathrm{cont}}_{t,n}$ and a new onset $p^{\mathrm{onset}}_{t,n}$, using the sigmoid function $\sigma$:
\begin{equation}
p^{\mathrm{cont}}_{t,n}=\sigma(f_{\mathrm{cont}}(z_{t,n})),
\qquad
p^{\mathrm{onset}}_{t,n}=\sigma(f_{\mathrm{onset}}(z_{t,n})).
\end{equation}
The observed-prefix state $h_{t-1,n}$ selects continuation or onset, separating continuing events from emerging precursors. Additional heads $f_s$, $f_d$, and $f_p$ predict the onset-offset distribution $\pi^s_{t,n}$, future duration $\hat d_{t,n}$, and prefix length $\hat p_{t,n}$, respectively:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\pi^s_{t,n}&=\mathrm{softmax}(f_s(z_{t,n})),\\
\hat d_{t,n}&=\mathrm{softplus}(f_d(z_{t,n})),\\
\hat p_{t,n}&=\mathrm{softplus}(f_p(z_{t,n})).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Softplus enforces nonnegative lengths, measured in one-minute windows. Prefix length is uninterrupted bottleneck occupancy from the first future window, distinguishing sustained activity from immediate recovery.
The occupancy decoder predicts $\hat O_t\in[0,1]^{H_{\mathrm{label}}\times N}$ as dense temporal supervision. A precursor branch fuses local queue/stall evidence with neighbourhood context before occurrence prediction.

Order remaining time and cause are graph-level outputs. After $L$ encoder blocks, the final node states $H_t^{(L)}$ are pooled through mean aggregation and a maximum gated-node score, then concatenated with order-progress information. Denoting this operation by $\mathrm{AuxPool}$, we obtain a graph-level vector $u_t$ for the remaining-time and cause heads $f_\ell$ and $f_c$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
u_t&=\mathrm{AuxPool}(H_t^{(L)},\text{order state}),\\
\hat\ell_t&=f_\ell(u_t),\qquad \hat c_t=f_c(u_t).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The cause head learns the four rule-derived classes in $\mathcal{C}$ from shared historical representations.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:phgst-predict} combines encoding, heads, and decoding. Pattern centres stay fixed after training-only fitting.

\KwOut{station reports $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_t$; occupancy $\hat O_t$; cause $\hat c_t$; remaining time $\hat\ell_t$}
\BlankLine

\BlankLine

\For{$l\leftarrow 1$ \KwTo $5$}{
$H_T\leftarrow\mathrm{Attn}_{T}(H^{(l-1)})$\;

}
\BlankLine
\textbf{Phase 3: station-level and graph-level prediction heads}\;
$z_n\leftarrow H^{(5)}_{t-1,n}+E_{\mathrm{regime}}(r_{t-1,n})$ for the station-event branch\;
fuse local queue/stall and neighbourhood precursor evidence into $z_n$\;
\ForEach{$n\in\mathcal{S}$}{
$(a^{\mathrm{cont}}_n,a^{\mathrm{onset}}_n)\leftarrow(f_{\mathrm{cont}}(z_n),f_{\mathrm{onset}}(z_n))$ \tcp*{continuation vs.\ new-onset scores}
$(\pi^{s}_n,\hat d_n,\hat p_n)\leftarrow(\mathrm{softmax}(f_s(z_n)),\mathrm{softplus}(f_d(z_n)),\mathrm{softplus}(f_p(z_n)))$\;
}
$\hat O_t\leftarrow\sigma(f_O(H^{(5)}))$; pool nodes and order progress into $u_t$; set $(\hat c_t,\hat\ell_t)\leftarrow(f_c(u_t),f_{\psi}(u_t))$\;
\BlankLine
\textbf{Phase 4: event-consistent operational decoding}\;
$\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_t\leftarrow\varnothing$\;
\ForEach{$n\in\mathcal{S}$}{
select $a_n\leftarrow a^{\mathrm{cont}}_n$ if $h_{t-1,n}=1$, and $a_n\leftarrow a^{\mathrm{onset}}_n$ otherwise\;
  \If{$h_{t-1,n}=1$ and $\hat p_n$ satisfies the continuation rule}{
lift $a_n$ with prefix evidence and set $\hat s_n\leftarrow0$\;
  }
recover the longest eligible run $(\hat s^{O}_n,\hat d^{O}_n)$ from $\hat O_{t,:,n}$\;
merge the run and event-head estimates subject to the ongoing/upcoming duration rule\;
apply the global threshold $\tau$, the resource-type threshold, and the onset limit $H_{\mathrm{start}}$\;
  \If{station $n$ is retained}{
append $(n,\hat s_n,\hat d_n,\hat c_t,\hat\ell_t)$ to $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_t$\;
  }
}
\Return $\widehat{\mathcal{R}}_t,\hat O_t,\hat c_t,\hat\ell_t$\;
\end{algorithm*}



\subsubsection{Joint training objective}\label{sec:joint-optimization}

The learning objective combines event supervision, dense temporal supervision, explanatory outputs, and representation regularization. Each $\lambda$ weights its corresponding loss. Event occurrence and new-event occurrence use $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{will}}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{onset}}$, respectively; the remaining subscripts identify onset offset, duration, prefix length, occupancy, remaining time, cause, reconstruction, and contrastive learning. The auxiliary binary loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tpm}}$ supervises prediction of whether a resource receives a bottleneck turning-point label at least once within the prediction horizon. The full objective is
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}={}&
\lambda_w\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{will}}+
\lambda_{on}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{onset}}+
\lambda_s\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{start}}+
\lambda_d\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dur}}\\
&+\lambda_p\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{prefix}}+
\lambda_o\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{occ}}+
\lambda_{\ell}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{remain}}+
\lambda_c\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cause}}\\
&+\lambda_r\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}}+
\lambda_q\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{contrast}}+
\lambda_{\mathrm{tpm}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{tpm}} .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Numerical weights are given in Appendix~\ref{app:additional}.

The occurrence loss combines reweighted binary cross-entropy, focal modulation, hard-false-positive weighting, and an internal differentiable F1 term. Separate onset supervision is applied to the new-onset head. The start loss uses a Gaussian soft target over minute offsets, whereas duration, prefix, and remaining time use log-transformed Smooth-L1 regression. Remaining-time samples are further weighted by order progress.

The occupancy term combines type-balanced binary cross-entropy, soft Dice, and soft intersection-over-union losses. Cause classification uses class-weighted cross-entropy only for bottleneck-positive windows with one of the four supported cause labels. Future-feature reconstruction and graph-level supervised contrastive learning regularize the shared representation. Other implementation-specific auxiliary losses are disabled; their configuration names are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:additional}. With $\lambda_{\mathrm{cluster}}=0$, event prediction is not trained to reproduce offline k-means assignments.

Training follows Algorithm~\ref{alg:phgst-train}. Stage~1 jointly learns the shared representation and event-related heads using the Onset limit~$\leq5\rightarrow$Onset limit~$\leq10\rightarrow$Onset limit~$\leq15$ curriculum. Far-onset weighting and an enhanced false-alarm penalty balance warning sensitivity and false reports. Stage~2 freezes the encoder and event, occupancy, and cause heads, adds a per-workpiece rate term to the remaining-time head, and optimizes that head alone using progress-weighted loss. This specialization preserves the event and cause outputs. Numerical settings and the common initialization protocol are specified in Section~\ref{sec:exp}.

\subsubsection{Validation-controlled report decoding}

At inference, the observed-prefix state defined in Section~\ref{sec:problem} selects the continuation logit when $h_{t-1,n}=1$ and the onset logit otherwise. Prefix evidence can retain an ongoing event and set its onset to zero. Occupancy runs are united with event-head estimates subject to the duration rules. Resource-type thresholds and the active onset limit then filter reports; Appendix~\ref{app:additional} gives numerical cutoffs.

Validation selects the checkpoint and report threshold using the configured precision/recall gates and station F1, with the documented fallback when gates are unmet. Both choices are frozen before testing; the same decoder is used throughout (Algorithm~\ref{alg:phgst-train}, Appendix~\ref{app:additional}).



\subsection{Production-sensitivity validation protocol}\label{sec:sensitivity-protocol}

Forecast accuracy evaluates prediction against the constructed labels. An additional replay protocol examines whether label rankings align with production sensitivity, measured by the completion-time effect of accelerating resources. This validation is separate from predictor training and inference.

At a saved factory-model state $t$, each of seven workstation candidates receives a separate service-bundle intervention. Workstation processing and its associated worker service use a duration scale of $0.8$, equivalent to a $1.25\times$ service rate, for 1,200 model steps. Baseline and intervention branches start from the same saved state and random-number state and run to order completion. Let $M_{\mathrm{base}}(t)$ and $M_{i,0.8}(t)$ denote their completion times, measured from the same time origin, for candidate workstation $i$. The completion-time reduction defines its counterfactual sensitivity:
\begin{equation}
S_i(t)=M_{\mathrm{base}}(t)-M_{i,0.8}(t).
\end{equation}
Positive $S_i(t)$ indicates that temporarily accelerating resource $i$ shortens the order. Each label method ranks candidates using its maximum evidence over the last three fully observed windows before $t$.

Normalized discounted cumulative gain at rank 3 (NDCG@3) measures ranking quality using positive sensitivity as relevance; Positive Recall@$k$ measures coverage of the oracle's $k$ highest positive-sensitivity resources by the first $k$ candidates. Weighted Recall@3 gives the fraction of total positive sensitivity captured by the first three candidates, and Coverage@3$_{0.9}$ covers resources satisfying $S_i\geq0.9\max_j S_j$.

The fixed acceleration protocol assesses label relevance; it does not select an intervention policy.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:exp}

We evaluate whether PHGST-DM can address restricted observation scope and difficult bottleneck supervision to forecast bottleneck events, process causes, and remaining order time. Baseline comparisons evaluate these joint outputs; component studies examine heterogeneous multi-entity representation, process-informed supervision and temporal pattern clustering, and recurrent-pattern-guided delay-aware modeling. A case study follows bottleneck onset, persistence, and recovery. Together, these evaluations assess how the proposed designs contribute to advance warning and joint forecasting. Supplementary results, attention analyses, and computational cost are reported in Appendices~\ref{app:supplementary-results}--\ref{sec:efficiency}.

\subsubsection{Overall experimental settings and metrics}

All experiments use common data splits, forecast windows, targets, initialization controls, training-budget standards, and validation-based selection. Full and ablation models start from the same fixed parameter state; all selected models and report thresholds are frozen before testing.


Shared PHGST-DM settings appear in Table~\ref{tab:shared-training-protocol}. AdamW uses batch size 16, learning rate $5\times10^{-5}$, weight decay $0.05$, cosine decay to $10^{-6}$, and fourfold positive-window oversampling. Under the two-stage procedure in Section~\ref{sec:joint-optimization}, stage~1 starts from the fixed parameter state shared by all ablation arms, unfreezes all five encoder blocks, and runs for at most 50 epochs with patience 40. Far-onset weighting doubles onset loss beyond 5~min and uses positive weights $9/10/11$ for the three limits; ordinary and hard false-positive weights are $2.5$ and $2.2$. Stage~2 runs for at most 20 epochs, selected by validation loss. All runs use seed 42 and provide point estimates rather than multi-seed stability estimates. Appendix~\ref{app:additional} provides loss weights, label thresholds, and decoding details.

The model and report threshold are selected on the validation split. Candidate thresholds are $\{0.45,\dots,0.90,0.94\}$; selection uses the internal station-identification F1 among candidates satisfying validation report precision $\geq0.80$ and recall $\geq0.70$. This selection score is not reported as a result; the reported event metric is the onset-qualified F1-score defined below. All three onset limits use the selected threshold of $0.94$ for testing.

For resource $n$ at forecast origin $t$, let $\hat s_{t,n}$ and $s_{t,n}$ denote the predicted and reference onset offsets in minutes. A strict report-level hit requires the correct resource and an absolute onset error within tolerance $\epsilon_s$:
\begin{equation}
|\hat{s}_{t,n}-s_{t,n}|\leq\epsilon_s,\qquad
\epsilon_s=3~\mathrm{min}.
\end{equation}
At the one-minute window resolution, this tolerance equals three windows.

Metrics cover bottleneck-event prediction, process-cause classification, and remaining order time. The reported bottleneck-event F1-score requires both correct station identification and an onset error within three windows. Upcoming recall uses future-event window--resource pairs whose final historical window is non-bottleneck; Ongoing recall uses pairs with an active final historical window. Onset and duration mean absolute error (MAE) are measured on correctly detected events, while union duration MAE and root mean squared error (RMSE) include missed and false events. Event F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall; macro recall is the unweighted mean of class-specific recalls. Next-window and 30-window State F1 describe the predicted occupancy over time, including recovery. Process-cause evaluation uses accuracy and macro recall over the four supported classes. Order-completion evaluation uses remaining-time MAE at the line level.

\paragraph*{Experimental findings.}
The experiments link heterogeneous resource observations to improved advance warning and process-informed supervision to the explanatory output. Temporal pattern clustering and prototype-conditioned interaction support event identification, duration estimation, and distant-onset warning, while the sensitivity pilot provides preliminary evidence of label relevance. Aggregate comparisons and station-level trajectories assess bottleneck location, onset, persistence, and recovery; per-device results show variation across resource families, and the remaining-time head adds order-level context.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Joint prediction relates the bottleneck interval to observable process conditions and remaining order time, combining a resource-level constraint forecast with an order-level progress estimate. Together, these outputs describe where a constraint is expected, how it may develop, and the production context in which it occurs. The differing model rankings across outputs also indicate that a shared representation need not be optimal for every target (Tables~\ref{tab:comparison-main}--\ref{tab:comparison-remain}).

The three component studies connect these forecasts to multi-entity observations, process-informed supervision and temporal pattern clustering, and prototype-conditioned interaction. Cross-resource ablations reduce advance-warning coverage; the matched-budget cause study improves process-cause classification with little change in station F1; removing prototype matching reduces distant-event recall. These are predictive associations and do not establish physical causal paths or propagation-time estimates.

Potential integration with manufacturing execution, warehouse management, and transport-dispatch systems would require mapping operational observations to the resource graph, as in integrated monitoring and simulation platforms~\cite{leng2023monitoring}. Such integration is not evaluated here. Appendix~\ref{sec:efficiency} reports model-side computation; scheduling quality, intervention-policy selection, and throughput improvement require separate closed-loop evaluation.

Evaluation is limited to the factory environment developed for NVIDIA Isaac Sim. Transfer across layouts, products, and physical factories remains untested. The sensitivity pilot assesses label relevance under fixed actions and does not evaluate forecast-driven production decisions.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This study presents PHGST-DM for dynamic bottleneck prediction in multi-entity production systems. A heterogeneous process--logistics graph integrates resource observations, process-informed supervision constructs event and cause targets, and recurrent temporal patterns condition delay-aware prediction. Experiments in the evaluated factory environment show higher event-prediction accuracy than the compared baselines; ablations support the roles of multi-entity information and temporal-pattern context in advance warning. The model jointly reports bottleneck station, onset, duration, observable process cause, and remaining order time. Validation in physical factories and evaluation of forecast-driven production decisions remain future work.

The objective in Section~\ref{sec:method} uses the following weights. Event occurrence, start, duration, and prefix losses have weights $2.4$, $2.0$, $2.0$, and $2.5$, respectively. The cold-history onset term has weight $2.2$ within the event-occurrence objective; focal modulation uses exponent $1.5$, and the internal differentiable F1 term has weight $0.8$. Future occupancy uses separate binary-cross-entropy, Dice, and IoU weights of $0.7$, $0.5$, and $0.35$. The reconstruction, supervised contrastive, cause, and turning-point/momentary losses have weights $0.2$, $0.05$, $0.1$, and $0.25$. Remaining-time regression has weight $0.5$ in stage~1 and $4.0$ during head-only stage~2.

The offline event CSV can retain a one-window event through score-event merging. PHGST-DM training instead constructs targets from the operational occupancy grid.

All state rules act on the original physical feature scale before input standardization. Let $q$, $B$, $S$, $U$, $I$, $R$, and $Z$ denote queue length, blocked seconds, starved seconds, unavailable fraction, inbound-wait seconds, route-delay seconds, and shortage propagation, respectively. At the 60-s resolution, a workstation has raw hot evidence if any of the following holds: (i) $q\geq2$ and $B+S>0$; (ii) $B\geq24$ and $q\geq1$; (iii) $B+S\geq24$ together with an upstream/downstream coupling ratio of at least $0.15$, $I\geq20$, $R\geq20$, $Z\geq0.25$, or $q\geq1$; (iv) $S\geq30$ and $Z\geq0.25$; (v) $S\geq18$ and either $I\geq20$ or $R\geq20$; or (vi) $B+S\geq24$ together with worker absence or labour saturation. An active workstation operating-variation flag combined with $U\geq0.5$ also supplies hot evidence. Worker absence uses unavailable fraction $\geq0.5$; labour saturation uses an activity cutoff of $0.8$ among present workers, with a presence tolerance of $0.05$. A gantry requires $B+S\geq18$ and either $I\geq20$ or $R\geq20$, whereas a transport robot requires $B+S\geq18$ and $I\geq20$; either handling type is also hot when $U\geq0.5$. When an operating-variation flag is active, evidence is retained only if $q\geq1$, $B+S\geq24$, or $U\geq0.5$. The resulting raw indicators are converted to occupancy targets by Algorithm~\ref{alg:targets}.

For correspondence with the code, the disabled auxiliary-loss switches retain the implementation names \texttt{score}, \texttt{will}, \texttt{mark}, \texttt{time-to-start}, \texttt{STGNPP}, and \texttt{offline cluster-head}. These names identify inactive configuration options; the active occurrence loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{will}}$ in Section~\ref{sec:joint-optimization} remains part of the training objective. The offline cluster-head weight is zero, so the model is not trained to classify the offline clustering assignments.

The prefix-continuation cutoff is $1.5$ windows. Occupancy-run extraction for event union uses probability cutoff $0.45$, with both ongoing and upcoming union enabled; direct occupancy-state evaluation uses cutoff $0.55$. The configured gantry and transport-robot report thresholds are $0.72$ and $0.68$, respectively, alongside the validation-selected global operating point. Threshold selection first prefers candidates satisfying the validation precision requirement and then maximizes the configured station-identification F1; if none satisfies that requirement, it retains the highest-F1 validation candidate. The checkpoint precision/recall gates are applied separately. If no gated checkpoint is obtained throughout training, the retained ungated checkpoint with the best validation selection score is used. Neither fallback uses test predictions or test labels.

Evaluation counts refer to forecast-origin--resource pairs, so a persistent event may contribute at successive origins. Overlapping windows are not treated as independent observations for significance testing.
