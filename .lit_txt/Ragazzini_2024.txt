A B S T R A C T  
Bottlenecks in manufacturing systems may significantly reduce their efficiency and productivity. Therefore, 
bottleneck analysis is a consolidated topic in Industrial Engineering, both in research and practice. Recently, 
traditional methods for bottleneck analysis have been enhanced with data-driven approaches, such as artificial 
intelligence and big data analytics. Nevertheless, their exploitation built on the full scope of technologies from 
digitalization is still not fulfilled. Indeed, the integration with simulation-based methods remains under- 
explored. This work aims to address bottleneck prediction leveraging on Digital Twin simulation capabilities 
to predict manufacturing system behavior. For this purpose, the work first offers an extensive review of 
bottleneck identification methods, inclusive of the ones based on Digital Twin. The main contribution of the work 
lies in the proposal of a novel Digital Twin-based bottleneck prediction framework with the end purpose to 
achieve performance improvements actuated through production control. The framework utilizes the Digital 
Twin for predicting and mitigating bottlenecks in manufacturing systems. The Digital Twin enables the simu­
lation of the future system behavior, while accounting for the current conditions. This insight can then be used by 
a bottleneck identification method to infer future system bottleneck. The information on the predicted bottleneck 
is eventually used to support production control decisions, by adapting the order release and sequencing ac­
cording to the predicted bottleneck. The benefits of adapting production control to the predicted bottleneck are 
evaluated quantitatively, highlighting how system performance is enhanced. By doing so, this research con­
tributes to bridging the gap between Digital Twin-based performance analysis and production control, providing 
knowledge and a practical framework transferable to researchers and industrial practitioners.   
1. Introduction 
Bottlenecks are well-known as limiting factors on the behavior of 
manufacturing systems. Often, they are investigated when a shortage of 
resources occurs with respect to the workload. They are a common issue 
in manufacturing and have a significant impact on the performance of 
the manufacturing systems. In fact, bottlenecks may cause a reduction of 
systems’ productivity, delays in production schedules, inefficient use of 
resources and, finally, increased costs. The identification and manage­
ment of bottlenecks are then crucial for improving both productivity and 
efficiency in manufacturing (Li, Chang, & Ni, 2009; Roser et al., 2001). 
Bottlenecks in manufacturing constitute an issue that has been 
thoroughly addressed by both scholars and industrial practitioners 
(Chakravorty & Atwater, 2006). However, with the advent of the fourth 
industrial revolution, new technologies have been used to improve the 
analysis of bottlenecks in manufacturing systems (Mahmoodi et al., 
2022). A new opportunity came out for reviving the studies over 
bottleneck analysis. Data-driven methods for bottleneck detection based 
on data acquired in real-time have been gaining attention as they can 
provide reliable and updated information (Roser et al., 2017; Sub­
ramaniyan et al., 2019). New technologies including artificial intelli­
gence and big data analytics were proven effective in enhancing the 
accuracy and effectiveness of bottleneck detection and prediction 
methods (Subramaniyan et al., 2021). On the whole, the current de­
velopments are well aligned with the characteristics of cyber-physical 
systems for the future smart factories, especially in relationship to the 
intelligence/smartness to adapt decisions to changing conditions in the 
light of real time data acquisition (Napoleone et al., 2020). 
Virtualization is another key characteristic for the smart factories, 
built on a virtual copy consisting of factory models leveraging on 
simulation capabilities (Napoleone et al., 2020). Building on this char­
acteristic, exploitation of the technologies from digitalization could then 
be increased. However, the integration of traditional bottleneck analysis 
with simulation-based methods is an under-explored area. In this

research has dealt with the prediction of bottlenecks by means of DT. 
Within manufacturing, the DT is in general defined as “virtual repre­
sentation of a production system that is able to run on different simu­
lation disciplines that is characterized by the synchronization between 
the virtual and real system, thanks to sensed data and connected smart 
devices, mathematical models and real time data elaboration” (Negri 
et al., 2017). As noted by multiple researchers, the DT is a promising 
simulation paradigm that has been impacting throughout different in­
dustries, from aerospace to healthcare (Barricelli et al., 2019; Rosen 
et al., 2015). More specifically, the evolution of the concept of DT is 
mainly related to the different applications that have been proposed 
over the years. The first applications of the DT concept were developed 
for the aerospace sector. Since the first publication provided by NASA 
(Shafto et al., 2010), which aims to mirror the life of air vehicles by using 
models to simulate the interactions of the vehicle with the real world, 
the research on DT has extended to industrial engineering arena, in 
particular to manufacturing applications considering different uses. 
Amongst others, these include production performance prediction 
(Reifsnider & Majumdar, 2013), virtual commissioning (Schluse et al., 
2018), support to decision-making and optimization of the design pro­
cess (Gabor et al., 2016), and virtual testing as DT provides a virtual 
environment that can be used for tests while avoiding any impact on 
physical systems (Eckhart & Ekelhart, 2018). 
According to its inherent properties, the DT could be adopted to 
enhance bottleneck analysis. By using the DT to simulate the behavior of 
a manufacturing system based on current condition information, the 
occurrence of bottlenecks is predicted to make informed production 
control decisions. Thus, this work aims to address the gaps in the current 
research, related to the scarcity of methods to predict bottlenecks by 
means of DT and to the exploitation of such prediction for an improved 
control of the production processes. To overcome these gaps, the main 
contribution of this work lies in the development of a DT-based method 
for predicting bottlenecks and mitigating their effects by means of an 
effective production control. 
The document is organized as follows. Section 2 states the objectives 
of the present work, while Sections 3 and 4 review the relevant scientific 
literature on bottleneck identification and DT. The proposed framework 
is described in Section 5, while Section 6 depicts its laboratory imple­
mentation. Section 7 discusses the results of the experiments and Section 
8 finally draws the conclusions. 
2. Research objectives 
The adoption of digital technologies in manufacturing is pushing the 
development of new data-driven methods for bottleneck analysis. 
Within this digitalization process, even if the DT appears to be a valuable 
tool for improving manufacturing system performance, it has not been 
investigated yet to a significant extent. Therefore, this work proposes a 
novel framework for predicting bottlenecks and mitigating their effects, 
with the purpose to improve production control. Specifically, three 
different objectives are considered for the development of this work:  
a. A scientific objective, related to deepening the understanding of 
bottleneck identification methods, including the ones based on DT. 
In fact, this work aims at reviewing the literature regarding the 
existing methods and tools and providing a deep study of the use of 
DT for bottleneck analysis. This will help finding the current limi­
tations of the proposed approaches. 
b. A technical objective, which aims at proposing a bottleneck pre­
diction method that uses the results of the DT simulations for pre­
dicting the system bottlenecks. The method is setup in the light of the 
limitations identified from literature. 
c. An industrial objective, focusing on the benefits in terms of per­
formance improvements that can be achieved. To this last purpose, a 
new way to exploit the knowledge related to future bottlenecks to 
improve the production control will be developed. This will be done 
by dynamically adjusting the Order Release (OR) policy and the 
Dispatching Rule (DR) for mitigating the impact of future bottle­
necks, to finally enhance the overall system productivity. 
Building on the prior objectives, as overarching objective of this 
work a framework aimed at integrating bottleneck analysis with virtu­
alization promoted through DT is developed, in order to foster a new 
way for achieving production control in a close connection with 
dynamically changing bottlenecks (namely, shifting bottlenecks). It can 
be considered the main scientific objective of this work. 
3. Context and background 
This work is grounded on a deep analysis of the state-of-art in 
bottleneck analysis research area. Firstly, the available bottleneck 
identification methods have been investigated and classified according 
to different criteria. Then, the adoption of such bottleneck detection or 
prediction methods to support decision-making in production has been 
explored, particularly focusing on production control applications. 
Finally, the contribution to this research field of DT and online simu­
lation is reviewed to contextualize the proposed framework. 
3.1. Bottleneck definitions 
Scholars have been defining bottleneck in many different ways. A 
brief outline is hereinafter discussed as precursor to the insights on 
bottleneck identification methods (see subsection 3.2). 
Sadowsky and Harmonosky define a bottleneck as a congestion point 
occurring when manufacturing resources required in a given time period 
are unavailable (Sadowski & Harmonosky, 1990), while Chase and 
Aquilano identify as bottleneck the resource whose capacity is less than 
the demand placed upon it (Chase & Aquilano, 1989). Other definitions 
are even more general, such as: temporary blockades to increased output 
(Schweitzer et al., 1993a), a facility, function, department, etc., that 
impedes production (Fogarty et al., 1989), any operation that limits 
output (Krajewski & Ritzman, 1990). 
Despite these definitions may provide a basic understanding of the 
concept, they appear too general to be applied in practice. Kuo and Lim 
(1996) propose other possible explanations for the concept of bottleneck 
in the light of its practical use in the bottleneck analysis: i) the machine 
whose production rate in isolation is the smallest compared to all the 
other machines in the system, ii) the machine with the largest work-in- 
process inventory in the preceding buffer, and iii) the machine having 
the production rate in isolation to which the system performance index 
has the largest sensitivity. 
Among these definitions, the option i) is referred only to the static 
system and thus lacks generality; the option ii) is more an indirect 
measure useful for the detection rather than a formal definition, also, it 
may depend on contingent factors; the last option iii) could be consid­
ered the most well-suited since, besides being a good definition of 
bottleneck, it provides hints to a practical adoption (Roser et al., 2014).

Due to the great amount of published works addressing bottleneck 
identification, presenting a clear overview of the different methods is 
not an easy task. However, some methods have a particular relevance 
due to their uniqueness or popularity, and will be thus discussed further:  
• Average utilization method. This method is one of the oldest 
and consists of computing the average utilization (ratio between 
working time and total time) for all the stations and simply 
looking for the station with the highest utilization value (Hopp & 
Spearman, 2008). Moreover, utilization can be also computed 
analytically as the ratio between arrival rate and the production 
rate. Usually, this method is used for long-term performance 
evaluation.  
• Waiting time method. This method considers the average time 
spent in the queue and identifies the bottleneck as the station 
where this value is maximum (considering the actual waiting 
time also allows for real-time evaluation) (Law & Kelton, 1991). 
This can be achieved also by observing the queue length instead 
of the waiting time, but the accuracy of the analysis is reduced if 
the capacity of buffers is limited (Lawrence & Buss, 1995).  
• Arrow method. For production lines, arrow-based method is one 
of the most discussed solutions (Biller et al., 2008; Chiang et al., 
2000, 2001; Kuo et al., 1998). The name is derived from the 
practice of drawing an arrow pointing from one station to the 
following one if the blocking probability of the former is greater 
than the starvation probability of the latter and vice versa. In this 
way, it is possible to identify the bottleneck that is the station 
with only entering arrows. This solution is extremely easy to 
apply, but it is valid only for line configuration and can be applied 
only to detect past bottleneck with limited accuracy.  
• Turning points method. It considers blocking and starvation 
data only. The bottleneck is defined as the station where the trend 
of blocking and starvation changes from blocking being higher 
than starvation to starvation being higher than blocking (Li & Li, 
2009; Li, Chang, Ni, et al., 2009). Again, the use is limited to 
production lines.  
• Active period method. It was developed by Roser at Toyota in 
2001 (Roser et al., 2001). The key idea is that the bottleneck is the 
station that is active for the longest consecutive duration. The 
term “active” refers to the active states, defined as those resource 
states that may cause other stations to wait (Roser et al., 2001). 
For example, a resource in working state may cause another 
resource to be idle waiting for a part, while a resource in a failed 
state may lead to blocking phenomena upstream in the system. 
Conversely, inactive states are associated with waiting for the 
completion of other tasks. Different variants of this method were 
developed:  
(i) the average active duration (Roser et al., 2001; Subramaniyan, 
Skoogh, Gopalakrishnan, & Hanna, 2016a). This variant focuses 
on a long-term bottleneck detection that exploits simulation to 
find the station with the longest average active duration (Roser 
et al., 2001).

findings from the literature. 
According to Yoon et al. production control refers in particular to 
two decisions: when to release an order into the manufacturing system, 
and which product to release, referring to releasing and sequencing 
decisions, respectively (Yoon et al., 2014). 
Regarding the Order Release (OR), the most popular production 
control policy relying on the concept of bottleneck is the Drum-Buffer- 
Rope (DBR) (Fernandes et al., 2014). 
DBR is derived explicitly from the Theory of Constraints as described 
by Goldratt and Cox in their book (Goldratt & Cox, 1984) and it is based 
on the idea of setting the production schedule on the rhythm of the 
bottleneck resource, as its name suggests. The bottleneck is the “drum” 
and sets the production rate of the overall system, the “rope” represents 
the connection between the bottleneck and the OR system, and a 
“buffer“ is set in front of the bottleneck to compensate for the time 
elapsed between order release and order arrival to the bottleneck. 
Therefore, the idea is to identify the bottleneck, set a sufficient buffer 
upstream of this resource, and release a product in the system every time 
an order has been proceeding by the bottleneck. 
Workload control is a solution for production planning and control 
which includes a multitude of rules addressing also the OR (Fredendall 
et al., 2010; Stevenson et al., 2011). It relies on the concept of bottleneck 
for its implementation, and it has been compared to DBR as well in 
previous research (Enns & Costa, 2002; Fredendall et al., 2010). 
Generally, workload control limits the release of the orders with the aim 
of stabilizing Work-In-Process (WIP) and lead time (Thürer et al., 2011). 
Considering sequencing decisions, Dispatching Rules (DRs) are 
widely used in practice where manufacturing systems are affected by 
different sources of variability, as global scheduling might be less suit­
able (Gonzalez-R et al., 2010). In general, a wide range of DRs are 
proposed in the literature and scholars agree about their specificity, 
which implies that performances are strongly dependent on the system 
considered and its boundary conditions (Blackstone et al., 1982; Sarin 
et al., 2011). Different articles analyzed the performances of various DRs 
applied to a system with bottlenecks. For example, to mention a few: 
Chandrasekharan and Knut compare two new DRs considering the 
bottleneck named BSPT and PR-1, and compare them with Total 
remaining Processing Time (TPT) and Shortest Processing Time (SPT) 
(Rajendran & Alicke, 2007); Yoon et al. propose a DR based on the 
Theory of Constraints in (Yoon et al., 2014); Zhou et al. propose to use 
different DRs according to the WIP at the bottleneck (Zhou & Rose, 
2009). 
None of the proposed DRs has been proved to outperform all the 
others for every performance indicator considered (Kaban et al., 2012; 
Mittler & Schoemig, 1999). On the contrary, different DRs perform 
better in different scenarios, being deeply influenced by the system 
features. 
It is also worth remarking that most works consider only static bot­
tlenecks, often determined based on long-term measures such as 
utilization. 
3.4. The role of Digital twins for production control 
The DT has become a tool for supporting decision-making in the 
industrial engineering field, including applications in manufacturing 
such as: 
• real-time monitoring and prediction: it allows to effectively repre­
sent both real-time and predicted data (Delbrügger et al., 2019);  
• process optimization support: thanks to the high amount of data 
collected and generated, it is possible to better understand the actual 
process conditions and decide how to improve process quality (J. Liu 
et al., 2019); 
• production planning: DT can be used also to support dynamic plan­
ning activity, thanks to information coming from sensor data, 
simulation data, and EIS (Enterprise Information System) data, and 
allows to test production plans on a Virtual Shop-floor (Tao, 2017). 
As underlined by Negri et al. (Negri et al., 2017), research on DT in 
manufacturing is an evolution of the already ongoing research stream 
about Virtual Factories (VF), which are defined as the digitalization of 
the plant integrated with the real system, coming in help to the pro­
duction during all the life cycle of the asset (Sacco et al., 2016). 
Relying on the characteristics of virtualization, production control 
seems a promising application area for research on DT. Herein, the use of 
DT can deal with the fact that manufacturing systems need to perform 
pre-scheduled operations and to react to disturbances over time. The DT 
can indeed support intelligent control thanks to a real-time perception 
through data collected from a dynamic environment, and by timely 
implementing smart control strategy in complex contexts. Notwith­
standing this potential, many published research works remain on a 
theoretical level or, at least, do not suffice to quantify the benefits 
related to the use of a DT in a manufacturing system in the full range of 
production planning and control. Therefore, the development of prac­
tical applications is required for this kind of analysis and evaluation. 
More specifically, it is evident that the use of DT to forecast future 
system behavior takes advantage of big data predictions of a 
manufacturing system state mainly for production planning (Uhlemann 
et al., 2017) or for the machining process planning (M. Liu et al., 2020). 
In this way, the DT is limited in its real-time component, and is reduced 
to a more traditional simulation approach. 
On the other hand, production control may take advantage of real- 
time capability to adjust manufacturing during execution (Bouslah 
et al., 2013; Cardin et al., 2017). Since one of the main characteristics of 
the DT is to mirror the current situation of the physical system (Negri 
et al., 2017), it should be possible to exploit the real-time capability by 
using simulation to dynamically predict the system behavior in the 
short-term. This application of DT for manufacturing systems could be 
particularly useful for controlling production as rigid production control 
policies and time-invariant strategies are incapable of dealing with the 
high variability and the frequent changes that characterize the shop 
floors. 
Examples of this solution can be found both in May et al. and in 
Ragazzini et al. (May et al., 2021; Ragazzini et al., 2022), where DT is 
used to compare different DRs, or in Ragazzini (Ragazzini et al., 2021), 
where DT is used to dynamically evaluate the optimal number of cards 
for a CONWIP system. These solutions consist in periodically running 
short-term simulations based on up-to-the-minute information 
measured on the real system and automatically collected by the DT, with 
the purpose to achieve simulation results useful to have a glimpse of 
different near-future system behaviors. This finally enables to evaluate 
the impact of production control decisions to choose the best 
alternatives. 
Similarly, other authors use (near) real-time DT simulations to 
address the disruptions in the production process in a reactive way. In 
fact, simulation results support the prompt reallocation of resources, 
which allows to reduce the impact of disturbances on system produc­
tivity (H. Zhang et al., 2018; Zhuang et al., 2018). 
Even if such solutions are promising and coherent with the contri­
bution addressed by this work, they seem to be developed as case- 
dependent approaches. Therefore, they do not provide a modelling 
methodology or a framework that could be transferred and exploited in a 
more general application.

4.2. Knowledge gaps in the scientific literature 
Building on the evidences from the initial literature review phase 
(previous section 3) and the systematic literature review focused on the 
DT applications with bottleneck analysis (this section 4), a few relevant 
knowledge gaps can be highlighted. 
In fact, despite the abundance of works related to bottleneck anal­
ysis, most of them relate to long-term steady-state system behavior and 
are thus commonly based on theoretical methods or long-term data 
analysis. Only recently the interest of the scientific community has been 
moving towards the detection of dynamic phenomena such as shifting 
bottleneck (Lai et al., 2021; Subramaniyan, Skoogh, Gopalakrishnan, 
Salomonsson, et al., 2016). This can be supported by digital technologies 
allowing to retrieve and process data in (quasi) real-time, permitting an 
analysis of the actual state of the manufacturing system. Furthermore, 
current research includes the development of predictive capabilities, 
which implies anticipating bottlenecks before their occurrence by 
addressing the prediction of the future bottlenecks in the system, rather 
than the detection of current ones. However, the developments in this 
direction are still at a rather early stage. 
While the interest in bottleneck prediction methods is growing, two 
areas of research appear largely unexplored. They can be related to the 
adoption of DT as a means towards the exploitation of virtualization: 
a. DT-based bottleneck prediction. Within bottleneck-related litera­
ture, the use of DT as an online simulation model has not been 
explored yet. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, only two 
research works have dealt with bottleneck identification going 
beyond static analysis by means of DT (Kumbhar et al., 2022, 2023); 
however, none of them leverages on DT predictive capabilities to 
predict the occurrence of a future bottleneck in the manufacturing 
system.  
b. Predicted bottleneck-based control. Adapting production control 
to predicted bottlenecks is still an open challenge that could be 
addressed to enhance the performances of manufacturing systems. In 
fact, the additional knowledge that can be provided in (quasi) real- 
time in regard to predicted bottlenecks would gain far more rele­
vance if effectively applied to enhance the decisions made. Produc­
tion control is a promising decisional area, as it is initially evident 
from some works found in the DT literature analysis (May et al., 
2021; Min et al., 2019; Ragazzini et al., 2021, 2022; H. Zhang et al., 
2018; Zhuang et al., 2018). 
5. Digital twin-based bottleneck prediction and control 
framework 
A novel DT-based bottleneck prediction and control framework is 
proposed to cope with the objectives stated in section 2 and addressing 
the gaps stated in section 4.2. It is sought to be applied to deterministic 
production or assembly lines, and it relies on the possibility to acquire 
data from each workstation on a real-time basis. The proposed frame­
work is composed of the four interconnected blocks in the red-shaded 
area of Fig. 5:  
a. the data processing block. This provides the functionalities to process 
the data acquired from the physical system into relevant information 
to be used for synchronizing the DT. It is connected to the Pro­
grammable Logic Controllers (PLCs) and to the Manufacturing 
Execution System (MES), to acquire data from the physical world. 
b. the Digital Twin block. The DT is used to periodically run simula­
tions, after synchronizing it by setting its initial state as the current 
state of the physical system.  
c. the bottleneck (BN) prediction block. Here, simulation outputs are 
analyzed for predicting the future system bottleneck. This takes 
advantage of the DT resulting in information on the future system 
behavior, including resource states over time. 
d. the decision support block. Basing on the predicted bottleneck, de­
cisions are made regarding the release and the sequencing of pro­
duction orders. Indeed, the OR policy and the DR are adapted as 
counteraction performed after accounting for the predicted 
bottleneck. 
Data processing, bottleneck prediction, and decision support blocks 
realize the interface between the manufacturing system and its DT. 
5.1. Data processing block 
The first element of the proposed framework is responsible for 
elaborating data in order to obtain useful information about the current 
system status. It is directly connected to the physical system to syn­
chronize the DT with the current system state. Different kinds of data 
can be processed, according to the equipment and the Internet of Things 
architecture installed on the manufacturing system. However, the in­
formation strictly required to synchronize the DT are the current ma­
chine states (e.g., working, idle, blocked) and the WIP in each station, 
including information on the orders that are on the shop floor. This 
finally constitutes the input for the DT which will allow to synchronize it 
to the current system state. In addition, the set of orders to be produced 
must also be available, as it is required to simulate the behavior of the 
manufacturing system in advance. 
5.2. Digital Twin block 
The DT is exploited to predict the future bottleneck of the system, as 
its main capabilities include representing the actual state of the physical 
system and simulating its future behavior. The DT can thus periodically 
run multiple short-term simulations in order to continuously enable 
prediction of the future behavior of the system. 
The process of collecting data from the field and running simulations 
is repeated with a certain frequency and allows to constantly simulate 
the future system behavior. Once the current system state has been 
determined thanks to the data processing block, it is possible to use the 
information as input for the DT, which will therefore run simulations to 
predict the system behavior. Each simulation considers the current 
production control. 
The output of each simulation run must include a log file of the state 
of the resources during the chosen time horizon over the short-term, 
which is the only information required for bottleneck prediction (see 
section 5.3). 
Since the aim of the proposed framework is to constantly optimize 
the bottleneck control, the time interval trigger has been chosen as in 
this way a constant control on the future behavior is possible. This is 
performed over a rolling horizon, which means that, as the 
manufacturing system moves ahead in time, the described process can 
be represented as in Fig. 6. 
For this reason, two main parameters are considered when utilizing 
DT predictive capabilities in real-time:

costs. Other considerations are more related to the tradeoffs of different 
impacts:  
a. High frequency may cause stability issues. This leads to practical 
problems in the manufacturing execution as the production control 
decisions may change too frequently with respect to what is practi­
cally conceivable.  
b. Low frequency lessens the impact of the predictions. It relates to a 
low level of synchronization of the DT with the field, with the sub­
sequent impact on the alignment of predictions with the status of the 
physical system.  
c. Long simulation makes the average system behavior more relevant in 
the simulation outcomes, if compared to the actual one, finally 
making the field synchronization less relevant.  
d. Short simulation does not allow accurate forecasts: it is not based on 
an representative length of the simulation, to effectively draw 
confident conclusions in regard to the addressed decisions. 
5.3. Bottleneck prediction block 
The BN prediction block implements a detection method on the 
synthetic data related to future system behavior provided by the DT, to 
identify which resource will be the system bottleneck in the time in­
terval considered. 
Among the various methods for bottleneck detection described in the 
literature, the proposed DT-based method was developed adapting the 
“active period method”. The choice fell on this method for three reasons:  
• it is a data-driven method and therefore its implementation is easy 
and efficient, even with synthetic data measured in a predictive 
approach.  
• it can be used for bottleneck analysis (Roser et al., 2002c), with 
reference to historical and present data, considering data acquired in 
real-time (Subramaniyan, Skoogh, Gopalakrishnan, & Hanna, 
2016a), to make bottleneck detection; it is also suitable for coupling 
with a simulation model which provides synthetic data to be used 
over a broader time interval for future behavior, even in the long- 
term analysis (Roser et al., 2001).  
• it is independent of the manufacturing system considered. In fact, it 
relies only on resource state analyses and does not depend on the 
characteristics of the manufacturing system. It well fits also manual 
and transport resources, making it applicable for every system which 
could be modeled as a discrete event system. 
As introduced previously, the method identifies the bottleneck as the 
resource that has been uninterruptedly active for the longest period. 
Being it used in a predictive approach, the input for the active period 
method is the log of resources states describing the future system 
behavior that is generated synthetically by the DT simulation. 
The data used to predict the bottleneck are synthetically produced by 
the DT, exploiting the DES model updated with the current conditions of 
the shopfloor. Such synthetic data consist of timeseries describing the 
operating state of each resource (e.g., working, idle, or blocked) at each 
time instant throughout the simulated horizon. Simulation results are 
processed to identify which resource is predicted to be active for the 
longest time during the simulated time interval. Indeed, the active 
period method only accounts for the state of each resource over time to 
determine the future bottleneck of the system.

At this step, the information on the predicted future bottleneck is 
exploited for preventively mitigating its effect through appropriate 
production control decisions as depicted in Fig. 7. In this case, produc­
tion control involves two fundamental decisions: 
• Releasing decision (i.e. “when” to release an order into the sys­
tem). This is tackled through Order Release (OR) policy.

• Sequencing decision (i.e. “which order” to prioritize among the

Order release policy 
Through the analysis of the literature concerning OR, two policies 
were identified which may exploit the concept of bottleneck, namely 
DBR and the Workload control, as reported in section 2. The former 
focuses only on the bottleneck behavior, while the latter also considers 
the balance on other workstations. Aiming to focus the proposed 
framework on bottleneck only, and to avoid introducing other factors 
possibly affecting system TH, DBR was selected for the scope of this 
research work. 
It aims at optimizing the TH of the system maintaining at the same 
time a reduced level of the system WIP. The working principle is 
extremely simple: this policy consists in releasing a new order in the 
system every time an order exits from the bottleneck station. 
DBR has been criticized due to its inability to deal with shifting 
bottleneck, which made it inappropriate for some manufacturing sys­
tems. This is overcome by coupling DBR with the dynamic bottleneck 
prediction tool based on DT. In fact, DBR can be adapted considering the 
bottleneck as the one predicted according to the results of the simulation 
runs in the DT, instead of considering system long-term bottleneck, as it 
occurs in the majority of the reviewed applications. This could also 
improve production control by considering the future behavior of the 
system instead of simply reacting to possible shifting bottleneck phe­
nomena. In this way, a new order will be released into the system every 
time the future bottleneck finishes processing an order. 
Dispatching rule 
DRs are generally considered less optimal but more flexible and 
robust solutions if compared to exact or metaheuristic algorithms, and 
thus they are often adopted at shop-floor level (Aazami & Saidi- 
Mehrabad, 2021; Gonzalez-R et al., 2010). 
Therefore, after the releasing decision, the sequencing decision must 
be made immediately. A multitude of DRs has been proposed in previous 
research, some of which can help addressing bottleneck issues in 
manufacturing systems; examples include Earliest Due Date and Criti­
cality Ratio (H. Zhang et al., 2009). Among these, the ones that can 
account for bottlenecks are considered and two of the best known were 
adapted for the implementation.  
• Shortest Processing Time (SPT) was adapted by considering the 
shortest processing time at the bottleneck (SPTBN), to reduce the 
overall load on the bottleneck.  
• Longest Processing Time (LPT) is modified to account for the longest 
required time to reach the bottleneck resource (LPT2BN), which 
considers the sum of the processing times on every resource before 
the bottleneck.

The proposed Digital twin-based bottleneck prediction and control

effectiveness. This is done by evaluating quantitatively the improve­
ments in the TH values, which is generally recognized as a measure of 
system productivity. This could verify whether production control 
decision-making can be enhanced by exploiting DT-based predictions. 
To this end, the results obtained using a specific combination of OR 
policy and DR will be compared to search for performance improve­
ments granted by the DT-based bottleneck prediction. A full factorial 
Design of Experiments (DoE) was developed: factors and levels are re­
ported in Table 3.

7. Experimental results 
The main objective of this experimental analysis is to determine 
whether production control based on bottleneck prediction, through the 
proposed framework, is superior to the production control based on 
bottleneck detection only. Both the two bottleneck analyses are realized 
through the support of the active period method, used in providing 
useful information to enhance system performance. The validation of 
the proposed framework was conducted in a laboratorial setting. 
Therefore, it shall be noticed that the statistical significance of the re­
sults obtained may vary under different experimental conditions. 
7.1. Presentation of the results 
The results of the experiments are summarized in Table 4. For each 
experiment, the value for each level of the DoE is reported, and the 
average and standard deviation for TH values are computed. Confidence 
intervals for the mean with significance level α = 0.95 are also computed 
(these are also graphically reported in Fig. 11). The number of replicas is 
also indicated, i.e. N = 100. 
Experiments 7 and 13 are not reported: since FIFO and CONWIP are 
not depending on the current detected or predicted bottleneck, the re­
sults are insensitive to the bottleneck analysis; therefore, they would be 
the same results as experiment 1. 
Statistical analyses were performed to evaluate the significance of 
the selected factors on system performance. Table 5 reports the results of 
the Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). The resulting p-values are lower than 
0.05, which shows that all factors as well as their interactions are sta­
tistically significant. 
A graphical comparison of the TH achieved with different factor 
levels is reported in Fig. 12, where the distributions show the perfor­
mances obtained with different levels of bottleneck analysis, ranging 
from the “none” case, where bottleneck is not considered at all, to 
bottleneck detection (“present”) and bottleneck prediction (“future”). 
The impact of the bottleneck analysis method is evident, as the shaded 
area is shifted right towards high TH values for the bottleneck prediction 
when SPTBN DR is considered, whereas the effect does not become fully 
clear for LPT2BN DR, this DR anyway resulting in lower TH values. It is 
already evident that the impact of DR seems much higher than OR 
policy. For any bottleneck analysis, the OR policy alone does not appear 
to significantly affect TH. 
Two different statistical tests have been adopted to prove the effec­
tiveness of anticipating and mitigating the predicted bottleneck through 
appropriate OR and DR. The statistical analyses focus on pair of exper­
iments, to highlight the differences in TH performance caused by the 
adoption of different approaches to deal with the bottleneck. 
The use of t-test and paired confidence intervals for the mean aims to 
assess whether the difference in TH performances between two experi­
ments is statistically significant. Similarly, Wilcoxon signed-rank test 
and Common Language Effect Size (CLES) have been selected for a more 
accurate estimation of the differences in the TH results of a pair of 
experiments.

the case in which bottleneck analysis is not considered is likely to be 
associated with an increased nervousness of the system. In fact, the ir­

shifting bottleneck may deteriorate the TH performance. 
Overall, despite the statistically significant differences between the 
experiments, the effect of the bottleneck analysis on TH is not always 
straightforward. The selection of the DR seems to influence the TH more 
than the bottleneck analysis level. In fact, the five lowest TH values are 
obtained with DR = LPT2BN, while the top five are obtained for DR =
SPTBN (see Fig. 11). Nevertheless, adapting the control of the 
manufacturing system to the predicted bottleneck allows to achieve 
much higher performances, provided that an appropriate DR is chosen 
(SPTBN in this case). It can be noticed that the highest TH values are 
obtained for DR = SPTBN and bottleneck = future (i.e., prediction). The 
results also show a slight effect of the OR in these cases, with DBR 
appearing superior to CONWIP. The average TH improvement obtained 
shifting from the baseline which considers no bottleneck analysis to 
bottleneck prediction is over 10 % for DR = SPTBN. This also results in 
CLES values over 0.90, namely see in Table 7, the future CONWIP SPTBN 
versus none CONWIP SPTBN. This evidence testifies how accounting for 
the predicted bottleneck in production control consistently improves the 
system TH.

8. Conclusions 
This paper proposes a novel methodology for predicting future pro­
duction bottlenecks in a manufacturing system based on a DT capable of 
simulating the future behavior of the system. The insights about the 
future bottlenecks can be exploited for improving production control by 
enhancing traditional order releasing and dispatching methods. Overall, 
the contribution of this research can be summarized in three main 
points:  
• It explores the methods for bottleneck analysis, and it systematically 
reviews the literature related to the use of DT for bottleneck analysis.  
• It proposes a new methodology for dynamic bottleneck prediction, 
proving the feasibility of using the DT as a synchronized simulation 
model which provides synthetic data for this purpose.  
• It quantitatively shows the benefits of adapting production control 
according to the predicted bottleneck. 
As overall contribution, the methodology is built upon a novel 
framework which, through the integration of field data processing, DT 
simulation and bottleneck prediction, allows to counteract, through 
production control, the phenomena of shifting bottlenecks through time. 
In particular, the DT consists of a simulation model that is synchronized 
with the manufacturing system, establishing an information flow from 
the system to the DT and, then, through order releasing and dispatching, 
back to the system. By dynamically adapting the control decisions to the 
predictions provided by the DT, the framework allows to achieve key 
performance improvements. 
The findings of this study have several implications for researchers 
and industrial practitioners, since this work contributes to developing a 
new application of the DT for addressing a common issue in 
manufacturing systems. The contribution to knowledge which this work 
aims to provide is twofold.  
(i) First, the use of DT based on DES to simulate system behavior and 
predict its future bottlenecks is a novelty per se.  
(ii) Moreover, compared to previous research where the DT is used 
for bottleneck analysis, this work allows to provide feedback to 
the system itself. This means closing the loop between the DT and 
its physical counterpart by returning useful information used to 
control the system and improve its performances. By exploiting 
the information provided by the DT, some of the limitations of OR 
and DR are overcome. 
Regarding the second contribution, it is worth remarking the evi­
dence currently gained from experiments. Indeed, adapting the DR to 
the predicted bottleneck provides a more effective way of performance 
improvement, whenever a proper DR is selected (i.e. SPT); whereas 
considering the shifting bottleneck may allow achieving even higher 
performances with DBR OR policy, even if this appears to reinforce the 
gains already achieved through the proper DR. These are the current 
evidences, as emerged in the manufacturing system selected as testbed. 
According to the authors’ understanding, its features may be a possible 
reason that somehow limited the impact of OR policy, to be investigated 
in future works. 
As bottlenecks are particularly critical in real manufacturing sys­
tems, this work could be also beneficial for practitioners. Predicting the 
bottleneck in advance instead of detecting it, even in real-time, provides 
a valuable piece of information that could be exploited in several ways. 
Being able to mitigate the bottleneck through appropriate production 
control decisions, is even more important to grant improved 
productivity. 
The study has the following limitations. The validation of the pro­
posed framework is performed on a laboratory environment, not 
covering possible implementation challenges which may emerge in 
more complex industrial scenarios. This is left for future developments 
of the research, which should study the application of the proposed 
framework within real manufacturing systems. Second, the experiments 
only considered few OR policies and DRs, neglecting other possibly more 
efficient production control methods that could show further responses 
to the inclusion of the DT-based approach to bottleneck prediction on 
the system TH. However, the aim of the experimental campaign was 
only to prove that it is possible to improve system performances (TH in 
this case) by adapting production control to account for the predicted 
bottleneck. Since this exploratory work has shown promising results, 
there is even increased interest in investigating this topic in further 
research work. This should happen also investigating it in different 
configurations of the manufacturing system. 
This study opens the path to many possible interesting future studies. 
First, the integration of methods for bottleneck analysis with the 
assessment of impacts on company performances (economic, environ­
mental and social), based on the results of the DT, may be further 
analyzed. As Artificial Intelligence approaches to bottleneck prediction 
are developed, their application to synthetic data to predict future 
bottlenecks may be investigated, opening the door to many intriguing 
possibilities. Lastly, bottleneck prediction based on DT should be 
applied to more complex manufacturing systems to further evaluate its 
effectiveness. This also paves the way for new approaches to production 
control based on predicted bottlenecks, inspired from the present work.
