1. Introduction
This study outlines a new approach for predicting
throughput bottlenecks in high-variety shops with com-
plex routings. Throughput is defined as the rate at which
parts are produced. It is further assumed that there
is demand for the produced parts (Hopp and Spear-
man 2011). In this case, increasing throughput has a
direct positive impact on firm performance, while at the
same time firm performance is constraint by the so-
called throughput bottleneck, which is defined as the
workstation (or resource) that has the largest impact
on overall system performance (Kuo, Lim, and Meerkov
1996; Roser, Nakano, and Tanaka 2001; 2002; Li 2009;
Thürer et al. 2022). Managers in practice are conse-
quently concerned with throughput bottlenecks (Sub-
ramaniyan et al. 2021), and a large literature emerged
on methods for bottleneck detection (e.g. Lawrence and
Buss 1994; Kuo, Lim, and Meerkov 1996; Roser, Nakano,
and Tanaka 2001; 2002; 2003; Roser and Nakano 2015;
Li et al., 2009; Thürer et al. 2022) and bottleneck predic-
tion (e.g. Li 2009; 2011; Cao et al. 2012; Subramaniyan

recent studies on lateness bottlenecks (e.g. Fang et al.

use the term bottleneck, then we refer to throughput

Quickly and accurately identifying throughput bot-
tlenecks is specifically important in contexts where
throughput bottlenecks shift, causing throughput fluctu-
ations across production runs. For example, a through-
put fluctuation between 475 and 800 production batches
was reported in a real-world semiconductor manufac-

world practice, and due to the stochastic nature of
dynamic shops, there is no guarantee that the detected
throughput bottleneck, which is based on histori-

proactive decisions predicting the future throughput

bottlenecks, specifically in shops with frequent demand
changes and various stochastic disturbances (Thürer et al.

If a shift of bottleneck can be predicted before it
occurs, then it is possible to counteract this change or
to prevent a shift (Roser, Lorentzen, and Deuse 2018).
Reacting to the wrong bottleneck however will either
have no impact on throughput (in the case the station
which is reacted on is downstream of the actual bottle-
neck) or increase the severity of the bottleneck (in the
case the station which is reacted on is upstream of the bot-
tleneck). In both cases, cost and resources for the reaction
will not lead to the desired results. The literature, how-
ever, has mainly focused on how to detect throughput
bottlenecks, and only few studies focus on throughput
bottleneck prediction.
Shifting bottlenecks are an inevitable result of variabil-
ity or randomness in the production system (Lawrence
and Buss 1994). With the increase of stochastic dynamics,
such as machine breakdowns, setups, reworks, product
mixes, operator availability, batching and fluctuation in
process time and arrival intervals (Hopp and Spearman
2011), the position of throughput bottlenecks in the pro-
duction system may continuously shift over time. Exist-
ing studies on throughput bottleneck prediction have
consequently focused on the temporal features in time
series (e.g. Lai, Shui, and Ni 2018; Huang et al. 2019; Fang
et al. 2020). But in shops with more complex routings
shifting bottlenecks are determined by spatial–temporal
features, i.e. a temporal dimension of workstation sta-
tus and an inherent spatial correlation among worksta-
tions determined by the routing of jobs. The real-time
production data has both, temporal features, i.e. work-
stations states as a time series, and spatial features, i.e.
different workstation dependencies caused by the rout-
ing properties of the mix of jobs currently on the shop
floor. In response, a new integrated temporal unidirec-
tional and spatial bidirectional long short-term memory
(TUSB-LSTM) neural network structure is presented in
this study.
The spatial–temporal LSTM neural network can cap-
ture non-linear features in both temporal and spatial
dimensions, which gives it an advantage compared with
traditional LSTM in the context of, for example, air qual-
ity forecast (Shi et al. 2021) and destination prediction
(Xu et al. 2021). However, existing ST-LSTM structures
were constructed by combining both the output of the
temporal and the spatial LSTM in a fully connected layer.
This may lead to wrong outcomes because the current
spatial status in the time series can only affect the cor-
responding current features. It has no direct influence on
other timeslots. This study therefore combines the spa-
tial and temporal features in a single integrated recurrent
neural network (RNN) structure. More specifically, an
LSTM neural network is used as first (basement) learning
layer to extract the temporal non-linear features from a
time series, and a BiLSTM neural network is used as sec-
ond (integrating) learning layer to capture the spatial fea-
tures (upstream and downstream) across workstations.
An ‘Integrated Input Block’ is designed as integrating
mechanism to integrate the spatial BiLSTM into the tem-
poral LSTM at the corresponding timeslot. This realises
alignment of ‘temporal–spatial feature pairs’ both in tem-
poral and spatial dimensions. The performance of the
proposed model is assessed and compared against a set of
baseline models using data from a discrete even simula-
tion of a pure job shop with stochastic routing, processing
times and inter-arrival times.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. A
short review of bottleneck prediction methods and RNN
neural networks is first presented in Section 2 to pro-
vide the backdrop to our study. Section 3 then states the
problem, before the new TUSB-LSTM neural network is
outlined in Section 4. Section 5 describes the experiments
conducted to assess and compare the performance of the
new TUSB-LSTM. Section 6 presents and discusses the
results. Finally, the paper concludes in Section 7.
2. Background
Throughput bottleneck detection seeks to identify the
current momentary, average, or shifting bottleneck. For
a review of existing methods, the reader is referred to
Betterton and Silver (2012), Roser and Nakano (2015),
Yu and Matta (2016), or Thürer et al. (2022). This study
focusses on bottleneck prediction. Throughput bottle-
neck prediction seeks to identify future momentary, aver-
age, or shifting bottlenecks. A review of existing bot-
tleneck prediction methods is first presented in Section
2.1. The literature related to temporal and spatial LSTM
networks, which will be used for the design of our new
method, is then discussed in Section 2.2.
2.1. Review of throughput bottleneck prediction
Classical statistical approaches can solve the throughput
bottleneck prediction problem for static shops through
various heuristic algorithms with ‘transparent boxes’.
For example, Li et al. (2011) proposed a time-series
based autoregressive moving average (ARMA) model
to predict the future blockage and starvation of work-
stations, which are then used as input for the turning
point detection method to predict the future bottleneck.
For more dynamic shops, Cao et al. (2012) devised an
improved adaptive network-based fuzzy inference system
(ANFIS) that uses data from a simulation model, such as

processing times, utilization rates, and buffer lengths.
Meanwhile, Wedel, Noessler, and Metternich (2016) pro-
posed an algorithm to predict near-future bottlenecks
that analyzes the increase and decrease of the buffer levels
to support reactive maintenance prioritisation. Finally,
Subramaniyan et al. (2018) employed a time series pre-
diction technique based on an auto-regressive integrated
moving average (ARIMA) method to predict the active
period of the workstations, which is then used to detect
the throughput bottlenecks. A major weakness of meth-
ods that use classical statistical approaches is the limited
complexity of the shop that can be analyzed. In fact, most
studies focus on production lines.
In response, bottleneck prediction methods based on
deep learning neural networks emerged. Lai, Shui, and Ni
(2018) proposed a two-layer LSTM network for the bot-
tleneck prediction problem in complex, dynamic multi-
job manufacturing systems. Huang et al. (2019) devel-
oped a throughput bottleneck prediction method that
combines deep neural network with time series analy-
sis based on normal or abnormal datasets for a flexible
manufacturing system. Meanwhile, and not in the con-
text of throughput bottlenecks, a gated recurrent unit
(GRU) neural network was adopted by Fang et al. (2020)
in their parallel GRUs (P-GRUs) neural network based
shifting lateness throughput bottleneck prediction model
for make-to-order shops.
The
existing
literature
on
deep
learning-based
throughput bottleneck prediction can be criticised for
only focusing on the temporal features of a single work-
station in a time series. This neglects the inherent spatial
features across workstations in the shop at each timestep.
Whether a station is an upstream station, a downstream
station or not at all connected to another station is not
determined by the facility layout. It is determined by the
routing of jobs. If there is routing variety then this rel-
ative position across stations may change, and gaining
spatial information becomes essential. A main challenge
addressed in this study, is to extract the spatial features by
using a suitable RNN neural network.
2.2. Review of the literature on spatial-temporal
RNN
In real-world practice, the data of many fields not only
has temporal features in time series but also spatial fea-
tures between different locations. This includes traffic
prediction (Wang, Chu, and Lin 2019), air quality fore-
cast (Shi et al. 2021), or destination prediction (Xu et al.
2021). Given its importance, several methods to combine
temporal and spatial features emerged.
Classical statistical approaches can be used to extract
the features from the existing related raw data, and the
processed data is fed into neural networks as input. The
biggest advantage of this approach is that the historical
raw data can be reprocessed by statistical approaches,
such as matrix transformation and fuzzy sets, before it is
imported into the neural networks. For example, Kong
and Wu (2018) propose a novel spatial–temporal LSTM
model for the location prediction problem that neutrally
combines spatial–temporal features into an LSTM, and
which is capable to deal with data sparsity by using
a linear transition matrix. Li, Hu, and Liang (2020)
present a Type-2 fuzzy LSTM neural network model for
long-term traffic volume prediction that introduces an
interval Type-2 fuzzy set to extract the probability dis-
tribution and spatial–temporal characteristics of traffic
volume. Yang et al. (2021) develop a spatial–temporal
LSTM to predict short-term outbound passenger vol-
ume at urban rail stations on the basis of historical data
by using an origin/destination matrix of the rail transit
network.
While calculating spatial–temporal features using
classical statistical approaches has advantages, it also sep-
arates the neural network into feature extraction and
model training. One of the biggest advantages of deep
learning is that the feature extraction process and model
training can be integrated into a single neural net-
work. In this context, stacked spatial–temporal RNNs are
adopted in various fields. Wang, Chen, and He (2019)
introduce a path-based BiLSTM framework for speed
prediction in traffic networks that models each criti-
cal path, and then stacks the multiple layers along the
temporal dimension, which enables the model to cap-
ture spatial–temporal features of the path. Meanwhile,
Yang et al. (2021) developed a spatial and temporal
LSTM model to predict short-term ridership of urban rail
transit.
Existing methods typically extract spatial and tem-
poral features by two different deep learning neural
networks independently, and then catenate the outputs
through a connection layer, or simply stack the neural
networks of adjacent layers, i.e. the output of the lower
layer serves as the input of the upper layer. However, the
temporal features of each workstation (i.e. state changes)
may have a direct impact on the spatial interactive fea-
tures across workstations in the corresponding timeslots
in the context of bottleneck prediction in real life shops.
Methods may therefore be unable to accurately integrate
the spatial features into existing temporal features in each
timeslot. In response, this study proposes a new method
to combine the spatial and temporal features across
timeslots.

3. Problem statement
There are many causes of bottleneck occurrence and
bottleneck shifting in high-variety shops with various
order types and complex routings. This includes inter-
nal causes, such as machine downtime, setups, reworks,
product mix, and operator availability, and external
causes, such as intermittent arrival, urgent orders and
market changes. From a shop floor perspective, these
causes may result in stations being temporarily over-
loaded. The load, or capacity, situation of a station is a
series of events that evolve over time. It is a temporal
property, and most exisiting studies focus on this prop-
erty. If stations are connected by the routing of jobs,
i.e. the sequence in which jobs visit each station, then
dependent events may occur, such as blocking and starva-
tion (Roser, Lorentzen, and Deuse 2018; Thürer, Ma, and
Stevenson 2021). Bottlenecks are the results of variabil-
ity (temporal) and dependent events (Goldratt and Cox
1984). This dependency is a spatial property, which has
a direct impact on the temporal property of the machine
state and vice versa. This interrelation of temporal and
spatial features along different timeslots is illustrated in
Figure 1.
To best of our knowledge, no throughput bottleneck
prediction model to-date has considered this interrela-
tion of temporal and spatial features, which is consid-
ered a major shortcoming in shops with complex rout-
ings since it may give inaccurate results. For example,
when there is blocking or starvation in the shop, the spa-
tial features between workstations at a specific timeslot
will directly impact the capacity usage of the dependent
workstation. However, all existing studies on throughput
bottleneck prediction have ignored the spatial features
between workstations, and only focused on the tempo-
ral features of individual workstations over time (e.g. Lai,
Shui, and Ni 2018; Fang et al. 2020). Moreover, exist-
ing approaches predict the throughput bottlenecks sep-
arately, instead of adopting a system perspective, which
leads to prediction results for different workstations.
Throughput bottlenecks are determined by their impact
on system throughput not individual throughput. We
therefore need to adopt a system view that takes the inter-
dependencies across stations into account, rather than
focusing on individual stations.
In response, this study develops a new integrated
TUSB-LSTM neural network structure. During the
development process we address the following problems:
How to extract the temporal features of individual work-
stations in the temporal dimension? How to capture the
spatial features among different workstations in the spa-
tial dimension? and How to integrate the temporal fea-
tures with the spatial features in a single neural network
structure?
4. Method development
Our new method subdivides the planning horizon into
timeslots and predicts the bottleneck of the next future
timeslot based on current and past information. It inte-
grates three elements. Section 4.1 outlines the Temporal
LSTM (T-LSTM) neural network. Section 4.2 outlines the
Spatial Bidirectional LSTM (S-BiLSTM) neural network.
Finally, Section 4.3 outlines the novel comprehensive
integrated TUSB-LSTM neural network structure, which
integrates the temporal and spatial features into a single
structure to proactively predict throughput bottlenecks
in high-variety shops with complex routings.

4.2. Spatial BiLSTM
A main shortcoming of a traditional LSTM neural net-
work is that it only makes use of the previous context (i.e.
forward features). While this is valid for unidirectional
temporal features, just extracting forward features does
not make full use of spatial information contained in the
data. The BiLSTM neural network (Graves, Mohamed,
and Hinton 2013) uses a different network structure that
allows for capturing the features both forward and back-
ward with two separate hidden layers, which are then fed
forward to the same output layer. BiLSTM contains a for-
ward LSTM layer and a backward layer as illustrated in
Figure 4.

5. Outline of experiments
Bottleneck prediction seeks to identify future bottle-
necks. Since these bottlenecks are unknown in practice,
we need to use a controlled environment where we induce
bottlenecks to assess and compare the performance of
our new prediction method. Using real-world data, we
will always be biased by the method used to identify the
unknown bottlenecks for further testing. Using a gen-
eralised model also increases the generalizability of our
findings. To appropriately test the detection methods, we
will introduce random shifts. The bottleneck is random
to avoid strong correlations, which would question the
usefulness of a general bottleneck detection method. If
there are ‘strong’ variables, such as production condi-
tions and demand patters, then these variables should
be used for prediction. In our study we look at random
events, as they commonly occur in practice. We seek
to develop a new method for predicting when a bottle-
neck shifts due to these events. The past information
used in our detection methods is consequently that the
bottleneck changed. The question is how long it takes
to detect the change and how correctly the change is
detected. This is what is of importance to companies
in practice, which often suffer from random events that
significantly change the characteristics of their produc-
tion systems. The simulation model and the creation
of the data set will be described next in Section 5.1,
before model training and evaluation will be discussed in
Section 5.2.
5.1. Data creation and preparation
To create the data set that will be used by all methods,
a discrete event simulation of a pure job shop, which is
characterised by undirected, random routings, has been
implemented in the Python programming language using
the SimPy simulation module. The modelled shop con-
tains ten workstations, and each workstation is a single,
constant capacity resource. The workstations have equal
probability to be visited, and each workstation is visited
at most once in the routing of a job. Operation processing
times follow a 2-Erlang distribution with a mean of 1 time
unit at the bottleneck. As in previous simulation studies
on bottlenecks, non-bottlenecks are created by reducing
the processing time. In our study this reduction is 30%.

=====PAGE 9=====
4444
L. MA ET AL.
An adjustment of the inter-arrival time instead of the pro-
cessing time would result in the same effect, although
more granular (Thürer, Stevenson, and Renna 2019). The
job interarrival time follows an exponential distribution
with a mean of 0.529 time units, which arbitrarily results
in a theoretical average bottleneck utilization of 90%
(Land 2006; Thürer, Silva, and Stevenson 2011). As in
Thürer et al. (2022), bottlenecks are shifted after the com-
pletion of a given number of jobs, in our study every 180
jobs. All stations have the same probability of being the
next bottleneck.
The throughput bottleneck prediction method deter-
mines the bottleneck for future timeslots. The results are
based on the state of each workstation, which changes
over time. If the timeslot is too short, the state of the
individual workstations may be too consistent over mul-
tiple timeslots, which would negatively affect the effective
extraction of temporal features. If the timeslot is too long,
the state in a specific timeslot may be inconsistent. Con-
sidering the characteristics of the machine state in the
time series, we therefore subdivided the planning horizon
into timeslots of 10 time units.
Simulation results are collected over 100,000 times-
lots following a warm-up period of 300 timeslots. To
assess performance, for each workstation we collect the
utilization US of workstation S during a specified times-
lot, which is given by the active time divided by the time
the station was available (active plus starving). The col-
lected sample (consisting of machine state information
and actual utilization) is divided into a training, valida-
tion, and testing set, with the corresponding percentages
of 70%, 20%, and 10%, respectively.
5.2. Training and model evaluation
The data of every 10 timeslots is regarded as a data sample
(i.e. sequence length), which is used to predict the work-
station utilization at the next timeslot as output. Each
sequence length is fed into the proposed model and the
predictive output is a utilization value in a timeslot. The
proposed integrated TUSB-LSTM model is trained in
Pytorch, which is a computing package based on Python
with powerful computing and automatic derivation capa-
bilities. The mean squared error (MSE) is adopted as
loss function to compute the error and gradient for each
timeslot applying back propagation during each itera-
tion. RMSprop is employed to optimise all parameters
to improve the performance of the model. The initial
learning rate and batch size are set to 10−5 and 128,
respectively. The loss performance of training set and
validation set is shown in Figure 6. Both training and val-
idation loss decrease steadily with the training epochs,
and they converge around epoch 20.

7. Conclusions
Throughput bottlenecks affect production output and
throughput times. Many managers in practice are there-
fore concerned about bottlenecks and seek for ways to
detect and potentially predict them. Bottleneck detec-
tion is more important in context where the bottleneck
position remains relatively stable. Bottleneck prediction
is more important in context where bottlenecks con-
stantly shift since it allows for counteracting the potential
impact. Although there exists literature on throughput
bottleneck prediction, this literature largely focusses on
temporal aspects. Temporal aspects reflect the relation
among stations if the routing of jobs is fairly directed,
and consequently layout (station position) and routing
(position of the station in the job operation sequence)
overlap. For complex, undirected routings the relative
station position constantly changes. A station maybe
upstream, downstream or have no relation to another sta-
tion at any moment in time dependent on the mix of
jobs currently on the shop floor. In these high-variety
contexts, both temporal and spatial features should be
considered when predicting bottlenecks. In response,
this study proposed a new TUSB-LSTM neural net-
work model that systematically connects multiple inde-
pendent workstations into a system by extracting the
temporal and spatial features between workstations. A
spatial BiLSTM neural network is used to extract spa-
tial features between workstations, which are integrated
into the temporal features at the same timeslot. This is
different from traditional stacking mechanisms applied
in the literature, and it allows for a better integration
of spatial and temporal neural networks. Experimental
results showed that the proposed TUSB-LSTM outper-
forms alternative models and provides good prediction
performance.
A main limitation of this study is that we neglected
demand features. The current bottleneck prediction
method considers temporal capacity features and spatial
job features that create the interdependence across sta-
tions. Future research could integrate customer require-
ments into the throughput bottleneck prediction model.
Another limitation of our study is the use of a generic
dataset. While this is required to control bottleneck
shifts, future research should reevaluate the proposed
TUSB-LSTM neural network in real-life shops using real-
life production datasets. The method is currently being
implemented at a Haier factory in Shunde in the Guang-
dong province of China. This includes the develop-
ment of responses to counteract bottleneck shifts. Since
the true bottleneck is not known, bottleneck prediction
methods should be evaluated in terms of response effec-
tiveness. Future research could also integrate bottleneck
prediction and bottleneck detection in a higher-level
model. The predicted bottleneck can be compared with
the detected bottleneck and the bottleneck prediction
continuously improved.
