A B S T R A C T  
Predicting throughput bottlenecks has received an increasing research attention in the recent years. Several 
statistical and machine learning models have been developed, with the objective of identifying future bottleneck 
locations for more efficient and cost-saving productions. However, bottleneck prediction remains a challenging 
task due to the complexity of manufacturing systems and the interactive effects from many impacting factors. 
This paper proposed the BSTAN (bottleneck spatial-temporal attention network) – an interpretable modeling 
framework to improve system bottlenecks prediction capability for complex manufacturing systems by: (1) 
characterizing line topological features using graph representations; (2) modeling spatial-temporal relationships 
and interactions between stations using graph attention network with gated recurrent units. In this proposed 
framework, the production line is first transformed into the representation of a weighted undirected graph to 
depict network structural information of a production line. An attention-based graph neural network with gated 
recurrent units is applied to capture both the temporal trends and station interactions to predict each station’s 
blockage and starvation. Future bottleneck stations are identified by analyzing the forecasted blockage and 
starvation distribution. To evaluate the model performance, an industrial case study is performed with data 
collected from a one-year production at an automotive powertrain assembly line. With the proper model con­
struction, BSTAN has outperformed both statistical and machine learning benchmark models, achieving up to 
15% lower root mean square error compared to the best benchmark method in predicting overall system 
blockage and starvation time.   
1. Introduction 
Throughput improvement has been a fundamental challenge and 
technological investment priority in the manufacturing industry [1]. 
With the disruption of COVID-19 pandemic, manufacturers have expe­
rienced unprecedented supply disruptions and extensive forced work 
stoppages, which further accentuates the need of improved system 
productivity and resiliency [2]. The Theory of Constraints (TOC) is one 
of the most influential methodologies to systematically improve 
throughput [3]. TOC highlights the need of identifying the most critical 
constraints impeding the goal and then systematically improving the 
constraints to achieve better system performance [4]. In manufacturing, 
such constraints are often referred to as bottlenecks. Timely and accu­
rate detection of production bottlenecks provides great decision support 
for factory management to allocate limited resources (labor, mainte­
nance, equipment upgrades) for the most throughput-critical stations. A 
multitude of research has been conducted in the past decades to detect 
real-time and historical bottlenecks [5–8]. However, despite the great 
benefits and use cases [9], these methods offer limited input for 
future-oriented scheduling and planning. 
Bottleneck prediction aims to predict bottleneck locations in a 
manufacturing network. Production managers can take advantage of the 
predicted bottleneck information to prioritize operational resources for 
bottleneck stations (e.g., part supplies and health inspections) to mini­
mize the process downtime for bottleneck stations. Bottleneck predic­
tion is still at an early stage of research with limited studies. The 
challenges of bottleneck prediction lie in the complex nature of 
manufacturing system dynamics:

(1) There are multiple input factors affecting bottleneck conditions, as

release plan, etc. [10]. To model such input output relationships, 
an adaptive network-based fuzzy inference system was first pro­

(2) The bottleneck condition at a station is correlated with its historical

(3) The bottleneck conditions at nearby stations are expected to impact

To address the above challenges, we employed the graph neural 
network method to build a bottleneck spatial-temporal attention 
network (BSTAN) to predict production bottlenecks. Graph representa­
tion is leveraged to effectively model topological bottleneck features in a 
target production system; attention mechanisms are introduced to learn 
spatial interactions between stations; and gated recurrent units is 
employed to learn time evolving dynamics in the system. The proposed 
BSTAN model encompasses three components: network representation, 
spatial-temporal modeling, and bottleneck identification.  
• Network representation: stations are mapped to nodes on a 
weighted undirected graph and construct edges based on in­
terconnections and buffer sizes between stations. Each station is 
associated with a set of bottleneck features extracted based on evi­
dence from past publications and expert knowledge.  
• Spatial-temporal modeling: an attention-based graph neural 
network is utilized to model interactions between stations to predict 
the future station blockage and starvation in the next production 
shift, as blockage and starvation have been identified as effective 
indicators for bottleneck identification [16].  
• Bottleneck identification: the turning point method [7,8], a 
well-recognized bottleneck detection method [17], is employed to 
identify future bottleneck locations based on forecasted blockage and 
starvation distribution in the system. 
The key contribution of this work is summarized as following:  
1. To the best of our knowledge, this may be the first paper proposing 
an interpretable modeling framework (BSTAN) that can learn both 
spatial and temporal dynamics to predict bottleneck in a complex 
manufacturing system.  
2. The proposed method leverages Graph Attention Networks to 
represent and learn spatial dynamics among production stations, 
while integrate Gated Recurrent Units to simultaneously capture 
time evolving dynamics in the system. 
The remainder of the paper is organized as follows: Section 2 pro­
vides the literature review of existing bottleneck prediction methodol­
ogies and recent progress of graph representation learning for network 
dynamics modeling. Section 3 describes the proposed bottleneck spatial- 
temporal attention network (BSTAN) for bottleneck prediction. Section 
4 illustrates a case study of implementing and evaluating the proposed 
method using production data collected an automotive powertrain as­
sembly line during a one-year study. The performance of the proposed 
method is compared and evaluated with existing models. Finally, the 
conclusion and future work are given in Section 5. 
2. Literature review 
2.1. Bottleneck identification and prediction models 
Manufacturing system bottlenecks are those stations most critically 
limiting the overall production throughput performance [18]. In the last 
two decades, significant work has been done for bottleneck detection 
and can mainly be categorized in three groups: analytical methods 
[19–21], simulation-based methods [22,23], and data-driven methods 
[7,9,17,24,25]. In recent years, data-driven based model has been 
closely monitored by the academia and the industry [26]. By learning 
from real-world production data, data-driven methods overall require 
less assumptions and statistical approximations, supporting more flex­
ible and extensible applications compared with simulation-based 
models [8]. Recently, researchers have identified that bottleneck loca­
tions often shift among stations during production [27]. However, 
existing bottleneck detection methods can only identify real-time 
bottleneck or chase back historic bottleneck information [11]. This in­
formation provides limited values for future oriented operational plan­
ning to achieve efficient productions. Therefore, researchers have begun 
to investigate possibilities to forecast bottleneck. 
Researchers have observed that bottleneck condition at a location is 
often associated with its past observations due to factors such as ma­
chine degradation and operator performance variations. To capture such 
temporal dependency, ARMA statistical time series model is proposed to 
predict future bottleneck indicators based on historical observations. Li 
et al. [14] proposed forecasting future blockage and starvation using 
ARMA time series forecasting while Subramaniyan et al., [15] proposes 
a forecast of active periods as bottleneck indicators. In addition, re­
searchers also observed that there are many factors directly affecting the 
production line dynamics, such as product mix, release sequence, 
operator shift, etc. Cao et al., [11] proposed an adaptive network-based 
fuzzy inference system to predict future bottleneck based on production 
inputs. To capture both input-output relationships as well as the system 
temporal trend, researchers have investigated using RNN (recurrent 
neural network) based models. Lai et al., [12] developed a two-layer 
LSTM (long short-term memory) architecture that predicts future sys­
tem blockage and starvation based on high-dimensional multivariate 
time series data. Fang et al., [13] proposed a GRU (gated recurrent unit) 
based method that forecast future lateness bottleneck. 
The main idea of existing work is to forecast bottleneck indicators (e. 
g., active periods, blockage, and starvation) in the future production 
period. Then, existing bottleneck detection algorithms will be applied to 
determine future bottlenecks. Therefore, different bottleneck prediction 
algorithms are proposed to forecast system bottleneck indicators accu­
rately, so that applying existing bottleneck detection algorithms can

However, current bottleneck prediction methodologies mainly focus 
on capturing the system temporal trend while separately predicting each 
station’s bottleneck indicators. They ignored the impact from bottleneck 
conditions at nearby stations [13]. There is a lack of research investi­
gating such interactive nature of production dynamics between stations. 
To capture such effects, the methodology needs to incorporate spatial 
information of a production system. Such spatial correlations between 
stations are non-trivial as there are various layout (e.g., serial, parallel, 
joint, loop) and configuration factors (e.g., buffer size) affecting the 
product flow. There is a need of investigating a systematic methodology 
to capture both system spatial and temporal information for effective 
bottleneck prediction. 
2.2. Capturing temporal and spatial features using graph neural networks 
Graphs are powerful data representations for abstracting complex 
systems of interacting objects and their spatial relationships [28]. Con­
sisting of edges and vertices, graphs have been used for modeling 
structural entities and relationships in a wide variety of applications, 
such as social networks [29,30], knowledge graphs [31,32], molecular 
structures [33,34], biological networks [35], and traffic networks [36], 
etc. Recent years have seen a surge of research and methodological 
advancement in machine learning for graph-structured data [37–39]. To 
model spatial correlations, traditional convolutional neural networks 
(CNN) can be applied for modeling the Euclidean space dependencies 
[40]. However, it is mainly designed for handling tasks with grid-like 
data structure, e.g. image classification or machine translation [41]. 
Graph neural networks (GNNs) have emerged as the state-of-the-art 
tools in a diverse range of domains [42], due to the ability to synthe­
size information within node neighborhoods, effectively capturing both 
graph structure and node features [43]. 
Conventional GNN models, such as GCNs (graph convolutional net­
works) or GraphSAGE [44,45], assign equal weights aggregating fea­
tures from each neighboring node for computing central node 
representations. To better capture node interactions, researchers applied 
attention mechanism to the construction of GNN layers. Attention 
mechanisms have revolutionized the way many sequence-based tasks 
are created such as NLP (natural language processing) models [46]. The 
core idea of attention mechanism is to adaptively attend to the most 
relevant features in input data to make decisions [47]. Graph attention 
networks (GATs) leverages masked self-attention layers to specify 
different weights to neighboring nodes, achieving improved perfor­
mance in many state-of-the-art applications [41]. GATs have been 
adapted and applied to several real-world tasks modeling network 
spatial and temporal correlations, achieving fruitful results. Zheng et al., 
[36] proposed a graph multi-attention network with an encoder-decoder 
structure to predict traffic conditions (traffic volume, speed) at different 
locations on a complex road network graph. Gao et al., [48] developed a 
hybrid model for long-term pandemic prediction using integrated graph 
attention networks with pandemic transmission dynamics, incorpo­
rating graph features such as demographic similarity and location 
geographical proximity. Compared with traffic networks or pandemic 
maps, production networks exhibit certain similarities. Each station 
(node), with unique node features, interacts with neighboring nodes 
spatially and correlate with its historical observations temporally. 
However, there are still distinguishable features in production networks, 
such as (mostly) directed material flow, common line structures, and the 
effects from buffer sizes [10]. From our literature survey, there is a lack 
of research investigating the adoptions and adaptations of graph neural 
networks to model production network features and dynamics. 
3. Proposed methodology 
This section will provide detailed framework of how BSTAN model is 
built to predict bottleneck locations in complex manufacturing systems. 
The entire framework consists of three main components: network 
representation, spatial-temporal modeling, bottleneck identification, as 
seen in Fig. 2. 
Network representation extract production layout and spatial 
structure based on physical understanding of a production line. During 
network representation, each station is mapped to a graph node and 
construct the edges based on its spatial positions and connection. Each 
node contains a set of bottleneck features, including historical station 
incidents time (blockage, starvation, cycling, and downtime) as well as 
production data (product mix, sequence, volume). Next, spatial- 
temporal modeling contains two parts 1) GAT learns relationships and 
interactions between different stations which captures line dynamics 
information; 2) GRU captures time-evolving blockage and starvation 
trends for each machine. Finally, for bottleneck identification, the 
turning point method is applied to identify future bottleneck locations 
based on forecasted blockage and starvation distribution in the system.

The Turning Point Method (TPM) belongs to a family of data-driven 
bottleneck detection methods, and is often benchmarked with two other 
detection approaches, the arrow method [52] and the active period 
method [53]. TPM can generate accurate detection results for both long 
and short-term bottlenecks when production incidents (blockage, star­
vation) data are available while providing intuitive visualizations of 
workpiece-flow dynamics. Recently, the original TPM has been modified 
and extended to model non-serial manufacturing systems [8]. 
TPM follows the heuristics that a bottleneck station will change the 
blockage time and starvation time distribution among stations. The ef­
fect of the bottleneck causes its upstream stations to have higher 
blockage time and the downstream stations to have increased starvation 
time. The following equations is used to identify the bottleneck stations 
in a serial line with n stations [7]:

4. Experimental study 
The proposed methodology is evaluated using a real operation data 
set collected at a powertrain manufacturing facility in North America. 
Due to confidentiality reasons, this paper only shows a simplified pro­
duction line structure (stations, buffers, material flows) by a graph 
representation and all numerical results are normalized. With the 
collected data, the proposed BSTAN model forecasts the next-day system 
bottlenecks. Its performance is compared with benchmark models in the 
literature by evaluating their accuracy in bottleneck indicators (starva­
tion and blockage time). 
4.1. System layout and dataset description 
The production line selected is a multi-job manufacturing system 
with 23 workstations. There is a variety of station types (manual, hybrid, 
fully automated) and line structures (loop and parallel structures), as 
depicted in Fig. 4. Two main axle carrier product variations are pro­
duced daily at the line. The buffer sizes between stations are docu­
mented and used as input to construct the graph adjacency matrix. 
The data used in this study is collected by an in-house factory in­
formation system (FIS) during 190 days of operation. The raw data have 
two formats. The first type is graphical incidents data which is generated 
automatically at the end of each shift. The second type of raw data is 
recorded in the format of time logs at each station level. Whenever the 
station has a change of product status (block/ starve/ cycle/ down), the 
time and duration of that incidents are recorded. This time logs can be 
used to extrapolate useful information including station downtime, 
blockage, starvation, cycling, as well as product mix, sequence, and 
volume.

For ARIMA model, the implementation of the original paper [14] is 
followed. The historical blockage and starvation data is first removed 
with its non-stationarity through differencing. Then the order of the 
ARIMA model is selected through the AIC metrics [56]. For Two-layer 
LSTM model, the original implementation and feature selection [12] is 
adopted. Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Square Error 
(RMSE) are two metrics used to evaluate their one-day-ahead prediction 
performance. Both BSTCN and BSTAN utilize graph representations to 
capture the additional spatial features of the station network. They 
follow the same implementation. The only difference is BSTCN was not 
applied with attention mechanism at the feature aggregation step. The 
prediction results for two stations (S4, S8) and the overall line (average 
for all station) are presented in Table 3. Station S4 is selected as it is a 
typical bottleneck location. Station S8 is selected since it a rare case 
when bottleneck prediction is difficult and will be explained in detail in 
this section. 
From Table 3, BSTAN model overall outperforms benchmarking 
methods by producing results with lowest residuals (RMSE, MAE). With 
an overall reduction of 8% blockage and 15% starvation prediction er­
rors (RMSE) across all stations compared to the best benchmark model 
(2 L-LSTM) in literature. For illustrating purpose, the prediction plots 
were generated for station S4 and S8 in Fig. 5. and Fig. 6. From Table 3 
and the figures, it is identified that BSTCN and BSTAN model generally 
outperforms other benchmark models. It demonstrates better captur­
ability and predictability by incorporating spatial features for the pre­
diction of S4 and S8 in the visualization. A rarely occurred exception 
when BSTAN doesn’t predict well when predicting blockage for station 
S8. Therefore, we intentionally selected this station for discussion. In 
usual case, station S8 rarely gets blocked by the parallel structure at S9A 
& S9B. The unusual peak at day 8 (seen in Fig. 6.) happened when S9B 
(manual station) had an unexpected operator stoppage for an hour due 
to unexpected human interruption. Such human factor is not recorded in 
the data so that cannot be captured by the proposed model. 
From the above figures, BSTAN model overall provides the best 
prediction accuracy for blockage and starvation time. Therefore, 
applying the Turning Point Method can yield to better identification of 
future bottlenecks. In contrast, ARIMA model cannot capture such sys­
tem dynamics as the modeling did not include input-output relation­
ships, e.g. the effects from daily product mix and product volume. 
ARIMA model predicts the trending of future bottleneck indicators only 
based on its historical blockage and starvation observations, therefore 
neglecting many critical features for accurate prediction. The LSTM 
model infers the future bottleneck incidents considering both system 
temporal trends and input-output relationships. It is shown capturing 
the dynamic changes of the system response. However, each station is 
predicted separately, and the lack of spatial features affected its pre­
diction capability. The BSTCN model incorporates both spatial and 
temporal features for prediction, and outperform other benchmarks. The 
performance of BSTCN is slightly worse than our proposed BSTAN 
model. Since neighborhood stations may have different impacts on a 
target station, by further applying the attention mechanism, the pro­
posed model adaptively focuses on the most critical features when 
obtaining embedding features for each station. 
As an example of demonstrating the spatial interpretability of the 
proposed model, we plotted the learned attention scores in the final GAT 
layer using heat map, as presented in Fig. 7. Each station has its atten­
tion scores with its physically connected stations. The higher the value, 
the stronger the association between two stations during the prediction. 
The left figure shows the heat map at the beginning of the training stage, 
while the right figure visualizes the heat map at the end of the training 
stage. Comparing the changes between left and right colormaps, it is 
observed that the proposed model can dynamically update the attention 
weights for each pair of stations during the training phase. The colormap 
can be read horizontally and the total weights at each horizontal row 
should add up to 1. The darker (blue) colors illustrate the station being 
assigned with more weights for the target station during the message 
passing (feature aggregation). For example, at the first row, station S0 is 
connected to two stations (S1 and S21). The colormap on the right figure 
shows that, at the end of the training, the features for station S0 are more 
heavily influenced by features from station S1 (dark blue) than from S21 
(red). It is explainable since there exists a large buffer (size >10) be­
tween S0 and S21 and it produces a cushion effect that reduces the direct 
impact from station S21 to station S0. Overall, the visualization of the 
attention score distribution can provide some interpretable reflections of 
the spatial relationships and interactive effects between stations. 
4.4. Discussion 
The proposed BSTAN model shows some considerable improvement 
over the existing methods in its predicting capability. The main 
advantage lies in two aspects. First, the proposed BSTAN model pro­
duces overall smaller forecasting errors (RMSE, MAE) as it can capture 
both the spatial and temporal dynamics within the network. Second, it 
provides interpretable attention scores that illustrate the relationships 
and interactions between stations within the system. Such results

highlighted the advantage of the proposed approach: first representing 
topological features using graphs; then followed by an attention-based 
graph neural network model to capture the station relationships. Such 
interpretable modeling method provides detailed information for field 
engineers to understand their system and help them plan actions. 
Moreover, it can be modified and adapted to many other useful appli­
cations in the manufacturing systems, as there is a diverse group of 
physical entities (e.g., human, stations, robots, AGVs, etc.) within pro­
duction systems exhibiting interactive relationships with spatial- 
temporal features. 
Within the bottleneck analytics, a successful deployment of bottle­
neck prediction should consider the following aspects: 
First, it is important to ensure the quality of data gathered. Industrial 
data quality can be evaluated using the 5Vs (volume, variety, veracity, 
velocity, value) [57]. In this study, we experienced the issue of data ve­
racity, which refers to the biases, noise, and abnormality in the data. 
There are five stations with abnormal missing data behaviors . These 
stations had to be either removed from the modeling or being imputed 
with substituted values. The data issue affected the overall performance 
for all the models trained in this production line. 
Second, it is observed that some outliers in the prediction was caused 
by abrupt downtimes or operator absence. These events can be unpre­
dictable based on the current level of data collection. As industrial 
systems can gather more data to infer machine health or operator status, 
this information can be fused with the current methodology as an input 
to further improve the model performance. 
Last, we can customize the modeling by adding additional features 
based on the local production line characteristics and configurations. In 
this selected production line, for example, production volume is an 
important feature and should be considered in the modeling. When 
planned production volume is low at this line, the operators would stop 
loading workpieces to the pallets at the line entrance. As a result, the 
production dynamics would change as the first few stations would 
sequentially enter the starvation mode, increasing their starvation time. 
Therefore, it is good to talk to the line experts to discover what might be 
some useful input features to consider when implementing the 
algorithm. 
Future work in the study includes integrating bottleneck prediction 
results systematically with a manufacturing system-level digital twin for 
dynamic plant operational support. The forecasted bottlenecks provide 
valuable insights for future-oriented planning for efficient production to 
offer companies competitive advantage during the digital trans­
formation. For example, the bottleneck prediction results offer insights 
for shopfloor resource prioritization to plan the operators on the shop­
floor around bottleneck areas. In addition, bottleneck prediction for 
system with limited data (size & quality) can be investigated. When a 
new production system starts to get deployed, there will be insufficient 
data to build up an accurate and robust data-driven model for bottleneck 
prediction. A potential solution is to explore the transferability of model 
learned from a source domain (system with abundant data) to a target 
domain (system with insufficient data). 
5. Conclusion 
Improving manufacturing system throughput requires a system point 
of view. This paper contributed a systematic approach in predicting 
system throughput bottlenecks. It improves bottleneck prediction 
capability by proposing an interpretable modeling framework (BSTAN) 
that learns both spatial and temporal dynamics to predict bottleneck 
indicators in a complex manufacturing system. The introduced BSTAN 
model transforms line topological features using graph representations 
and models both spatial and temporal dependencies by integrating 
graph attention network with gated recurrent units. The resulting model 
is evaluated through an industrial case study for estimating the next-day 
bottleneck indicators (blockage and starvation time) for each station. 
BSTAN led to more accurate bottleneck prediction results than the state- 
of-the-art methods, and the heatmap with the learnt final GAT layer 
provided an interpretable visualization showing the interactions be­
tween stations during feature aggregation. In addition, the proposed 
model demonstrated promising applicability to be modified and inte­
grated with other useful applications in the manufacturing systems. 
This paper also provides practical suggestions for how to improve 
bottleneck prediction use in the industry. In the short term, bottleneck 
prediction results can be integrated with manufacturing system digital 
twin for integrated dynamic plant operation support. Further exten­
sions, such as forecasting bottlenecks with data of limited size or 
incomplete information would be also valuable to be considered in 
future research.
