ABSTRACT 
 
Improving production system performance is a constant need and a priority for manufacturers. 
Planning effective operations to maintain high throughput and resilience during production is not 
a trivial task. Manufacturers are looking for systematic, reliable, and efficient tools to reach their 
production goals, in the face of increasing production complexity, unplanned disruptions, and 
uncertain customer demands.  
This proposed dissertation aims at developing relevant tools to support real-time throughput 
and resilience improvement decision-making in complex manufacturing systems. First, a 
methodology is proposed for detecting dynamic throughput bottlenecks for manufacturing systems 
with non-serial layout conditions. Existing data-driven bottleneck detection tools are well studied 
for serial lines. In this study, a systematic framework is proposed to extend a well-known 
bottleneck detection method to complex manufacturing systems, with loop and parallel structures. 
The spatial distribution of station blockage time and starvation time is selected as the main 
indicator for identifying bottlenecks, based on their reflection of production system dynamics. 
Second, the dissertation investigates the predictive modeling for forecasting throughput 
bottlenecks. The concepts of factory physics by Professor Wallace Hopp are applied to identify 
dominant features that collaboratively affect the bottleneck locations in a system. A machine 
learning algorithm (Long Short-Term Memory, LSTM) is adopted to effectively capture time 
series dynamics and handle high dimensional inputs.  Third, the dissertation proposes a framework 

=====PAGE 13=====
 
 
xii 
for resilience dynamics modeling and control. Disruptions near bottleneck locations can have 
significant impact on system throughput. A resilient manufacturing system is capable of managing 
and maintaining throughput in an environment with stochastic disruptions. This research explores 
how to incorporate the bottleneck detection model with the proposed resilience dynamics model 
for improving real-time production operations (order dispatching) to achieve higher resilience and 
throughput during production.  
The proposed methodologies are developed and validated alongside the support of industrial 
experts. For the bottleneck detection and prediction models, the effectiveness of the approach is 
evaluated upon daily industrial data in a major automotive OEM with a span over 1 year. The 
bottleneck detection tools implemented have made tangible impact at the plant. The resilience 
dynamics modeling and control is formulated with close cooperation with industrial partners to 
make sure that the solutions are relevant and applicable to their production scenarios. The 
simulation platform and the results are closely evaluated by industrial researchers and practitioners.  
Overall, the dissertation proposed methodologies with both scientific and engineering 
contributions. The tools developed in this dissertation, if deployed correctly, can provide 
manufacturers greater capabilities in improving its throughput and overall equipment effectiveness 
(OEE).

CHAPTER 1 Introduction  
 
1.1 Motivation 
Manufacturing plays a vital role in almost every sector of a country’s economy. Advances in 
manufacturing not only help economic development, but also improve people’s standards of living. 
It is estimated that by 2025, the overall global consumption will reach $62 trillion, twice its 2013 
level. An extra 1.8 billion people will be joining the current global consumers for manufactured 
goods, with the vast majority living in emerging regions that will require basic needs such as cars, 
consumer electronics, and machinery. This emerging market brings great outlook and thrust for 
improvement and innovations for the global manufacturing industry (Gambell et al., 2017).  
Despite a promising future, fundamental issues and challenges continue to affect the 
manufacturing industry; a major one is improving production throughput. Improving production 
throughput is a fundamental challenge and need for manufacturers. In a recent industrial survey 
sent to over 200 manufacturing leaders in the United States, higher productivity is ranked as the 
number one technology investment priority for manufacturing companies for the next decade, 
followed by faster responsiveness to customer orders and enhanced collaboration with customers 
and suppliers (Kronos, 2016). Improving manufacturing system throughput effectively and cost-
efficiently is not a trivial task. There are various factors affecting the overall speed of production, 
such as production line design, part

=====PAGE 15=====
 
 
2 
supplies, labor, station availability and performances, etc. With limited financial and time 
resources available for a production plant, it is essential for factory management to have a 
systematic approach to identify when and where to perform throughput improvement activities.  
The Theory of Constraints (TOC) provides a great road map for systematically approaching 
throughput improvement under limited resources. Dr. Eliyahu Goldratt introduced this concept in 
1984 and it has thereafter made a wide-ranging impact on the world of industrial management  
(Goldratt and Cox, 1984). TOC highlights the need to identify the most critical limiting factor (i.e., 
constraint) impeding a goal and then methodically improve that constraint until the undesired 
factor is eliminated. In manufacturing, a bottleneck is one of the constraints. Throughput 
bottlenecks are the stations defining and constraining the productivity of a production line. A 
multitude of research efforts has been made in the last two decades to quantitatively define and 
determine the throughput bottleneck in production lines. The main approach can be categorized 
into simulation-based methods, analytical methods, and data-driven methods (Moss and Yu, 1999; 
Chiang et al., 2000; Chang, et al., 2007; Li, et al., 2009). In recent years, industrial practitioners 
have been paying close attention to the development of data-driven bottleneck detection methods, 
as they are in general more cost-effective, have short development cycles, and support near real-
time computation.  
Today, an exciting wave of evolution is transforming the landscape of the manufacturing 
industry, commonly referred to as “Industry 4.0”. Multiple technology advances are converging in 
this era, including an explosion in the volume of available data, the achievements in data analytics 
and machine learning, and the accessibility of fast computing hardware and industrial 
internet/cloud. Under the “buzzword” of “Industry 4.0”, the clear goal is to address the 
fundamental issues of the manufacturing industry, including throughput improvement. Smart

manufacturing or “Industry 4.0” is no longer a “future trend”, but an active strategy and research 
agenda in companies and academia. 
Even though today’s machine learning applications demonstrate unprecedented accuracy in 
many classification and regression tasks, it is often accompanied by the worry and criticism of 
lacking enough transparency and interpretability. Many industrial applications cannot accept a 
“black-box” approach as the prediction output may have significant real-world consequences.  
This dissertation aims to propose throughput and resilience improvement methodologies 
combining the strengths of the latest data-driven analytics modeling with the fundamentals of 
factory physics knowledge and manufacturing system dynamics. Prof. Jun Ni’s research group has 
rich experience conducting multi-disciplinary research on manufacturing systems modeling and 
analytics. This dissertation stands on the shoulders of existing research to further extend the state-
of-the-art throughput improvement models for today’s cloud-based manufacturing systems with 
complex layouts and customer demands.  
 
1.2 Research Issues 
1.2.1 Manufacturing system throughput improvement  
Production systems are subject to random disturbances (e.g., unexpected down time, 
fluctuating cycle time, etc.). Therefore, the total number of products produced by a system is a 
random variable. Production throughput is commonly used to characterize the product volume 
produced within a time horizon. Production throughput can be normalized as the production rate 

=====PAGE 17=====
 
 
4 
(PR), which is defined as the average number of parts produced by the last machine in the 
production system per unit of time (Li et al., 2009).  
Much research has been conducted in the area of throughput analysis since the 1950s 
(Koenigsberg, 1959; Buzacott and Hanifin, 1978; Dallery and Gershwin, 1992; Jacobs and 
Meerkov, 1995; Papadopoulos and Heavey, 1996). However, exact analytical results only exist for 
the two-machine-one-buffer system and systems with infinite buffer capacity or without buffers 
(Dallery and Gershwin, 1992; Li et al., 2009). For systems with more machines, no exact closed-
form solution exists. Approximation methods are usually based on either aggregation or 
decomposition approaches.  Meerkov and his group studied aggregation method for Bernoulli, 
geometric, and exponential machine reliability models, and proved the convergence of their 
aggregation procedures (Kuo et al., 1996; Li and Meerkov, 2000; Chiang et al., 2001). Gershwin 
has made significant contributions in the decomposition method (Gershwin, 1987).  
To identify effective measures for improving the manufacturing throughput, many researchers 
have proposed methods for optimizing various areas of manufacturing systems to achieve 
throughput gain, including identifying manufacturing system bottleneck and systematically 
improving them (Roser, 2002; Chang et al., 2007; Li, 2009; Subramaniyan et al., 2018), plant-
level maintenance decision support system (L. Li, Ambani, et al., 2009), identifying throughput 
improvement metrics,  job sequencing and production control (Frazzon et al., 2018). 
Among these methods, a widely recognized approach for throughput improvement is based on 
the Theory of Constraints (TOC), introduced by Dr. Elyahu Goldratt in 1984 (Goldratt and Cox, 
1984). TOC suggests the key drivers for system continuous improvement is to identify and address 
the critical limiting factors. Much research effort has therefore been devoted in the last two decades 

=====PAGE 18=====
 
 
5 
to defining and detecting the bottlenecks in the production line ((Ching et al., 2008; Leporis and 
Kralova, 2010; Li et al., 2009; Scholz-reiter et al., 2011)). Accurately defining and identifying 
bottlenecks in complex manufacturing systems is still an important research problem in spite of 
extensive work performed in this area. Once bottleneck locations have successfully been 
identified, effective measures can be performed to improve the overall throughput (Subramaniyan 
et al., 2019).  
Successful throughput improvement projects can result in significant impact for manufacturing 
companies. General Motors (GM), one of the largest automotive manufacturers, documented their 
long-term project to improve the throughput performance started since the late 1980s (Alden et al., 
2006). The study highlighted the intensified automotive competition led to the need to increase 
throughput. Responding to the need, GM has adopted the TOC concept and developed internal 
manufacturing system simulation tools. Their efforts created significant value for the corporation 
with estimated savings and increased revenue of over $2.1 billion.  
1.2.2 Characterizations of manufacturing systems   
The building blocks of a manufacturing system, or manufacturing lines, include workstations, 
buffers, and parts or workpieces. A workstation (i.e., station, work center, process center) is a 
collection of one or more machines or manual stations that perform essentially identical functions. 
Buffers are used to adjust variations in the production process. A part is a piece of raw material, a 
component, a subassembly, or an assembly that is worked on at the workstations in a plant (Hopp 
and Spearman, 2011).  In the most basic form, a manufacturing system consists of a series of 
workstations separated by buffers, through which parts move sequentially until exiting the systems 
as completed end items (Alden et al., 2006).  

=====PAGE 19=====
 
 
6 
Real world manufacturing systems are complex, stochastic, and dynamic. One area of the 
system complexity is the different possible layout in a manufacturing system, including: serial line, 
assembly/disassembly systems, parallel lines, split/merge, closed loop systems, etc. (Li et al., 
2009). Examples of stochastic and dynamic characteristics are the processing time variation of a 
station and unforeseen changes, such as varying customer orders or unplanned station breakdown 
(Frazzon et al., 2018).  
Based on the properties assumed for the manufacturing systems, studies can be categorized 
based on systems with reliable or unreliable machines, and with finite or infinite buffer capacities. 
From another point of view, production systems can be categorized as discrete and continuous 
models. For the reliability modeling, the discrete models consist of Bernoulli and geometric 
machines. The continuous models have exponential and non-exponential assumptions. The figure 
below shows a categorization of production systems.

There are multiple types of manufacturing data collected from different resources and each 
contains various information about the processes. The first common type is the raw data collected 
from PLC, sensors, or controllers. These data often measure physical properties, large in volume, 
containing missing values, and are difficult to interpret without a direct knowledge of the process. 
The second type of data is processed data from manufacturing network/ systems that often contain

1.3 Research Objectives  
This dissertation aims at creating fundamental understanding and developing practical tools to 
systematically improve manufacturing system-level throughput improvement capabilities. The 
following fundamental questions will be discussed in this dissertation: 

=====PAGE 21=====
 
 
8 
1. How to diagnose a complex manufacturing line to determine the current and historical 
critical stations that exert the largest negative impact on production rate (throughput 
bottlenecks) using the production data.  
2. How to forecast the future throughput bottlenecks given specific production requirements 
and projected station performances. 
3. How to develop strategies to mitigate the impact of bottleneck stations for throughput 
improvement considering potential system disruptions. 
To address these questions, first, a data-driven bottleneck detection approach for serial line is 
further extended to handle complex layout conditions. The proposed model is not only capable of 
generating accurate detection results for non-serial configurations, but also able to provide intuitive 
visualizations with understanding of the workpiece-flow dynamics. 
Second, a data-driven bottleneck prediction methodology is proposed. Bottleneck prediction 
has been a challenging task due to multiple factors jointly affecting bottleneck dynamics.  In this 
research, a machine learning algorithm (Long Short-Term Memory, LSTM) is explored and 
designed to model the time-series dynamics of the system and predict future production system 
states and thus bottlenecks. 
Third, a resilience dynamic modeling and control strategy is proposed to improve production 
throughput and resilience when production systems are subject to stochastic disruptions. At the 
time of this writing, the complex joint effect of manufacturing system resilience and throughput 
has not been satisfactorily investigated. Based on the real-time bottleneck location and buffer 
states, this dissertation research provides a framework to quantify dynamic system resilience level 
and generate real-time dispatching sequence to optimize system performance.

CHAPTER 2  Data-driven Bottleneck Detection in 
Complex Manufacturing Systems  
 
2.1 Introduction 
Throughput bottlenecks are the critical stations limiting the productivity of a manufacturing 
system. With limited financial and human resources, manufacturers can take advantage of knowing 
bottleneck locations to perform cost effective actions for throughput improvement. Production 
systems are stochastic and dynamically changing (Gu et al., 2017). Therefore, the bottleneck 
location is usually non-static and will shift over time. Analyzing the transient or dynamic 
bottlenecks therefore brings significant values for production managers in practice.  
Li et al. (Li, Chang, et al., 2009) proposed to utilize blockage and starvation time as primary 
indicators to identify transient bottlenecks. This method, called “the Turning Point Method”, is 
one of the most well-recognized data-driven dynamic bottleneck detection approach (Yu and 
Matta, 2016). It supports real-time detection and provides a quantitative measurement of the 
severity of bottleneck effects. However, the Turning Point Method was originally defined for serial 
lines and therefore has limited industrial applicability for complex production systems. There are 
three challenges for extending it to complex lines. First, the terms “blockage” and “starvation” are 
defined based on direct spatial connection between consecutive nearby stations. When complex 
layout structure exists, like the convergence or the divergence of line, the blockage and starvation 

=====PAGE 25=====
 
 
12 
status become non-trivial. Second, in complex structures, bottleneck station can possibly exist in 
both the main and the branch lines. It requires a systematic method to determine the most 
significant bottleneck station for the overall system output, despite its locations in the line. The 
third challenge is to provide a method that can be easily integrated with available industrial system 
data and domain knowledge. Its working principles should be intuitive for industrial engineers and 
line experts to understand, so that with the correct understanding of the methodology, domain 
experts can make adjustment based on local line configurations. In recent years, research attention 
in data-driven modeling for manufacturing systems has been attracted to include such domain 
knowledge to generate models with robustness and explainability (Wang et al., 2020; H. Zhang et 
al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2021). 
Therefore, the goal of this study is to improve the current data-driven dynamic bottleneck 
detection methods, “the Turning Point Method”, so that it adapts to a wider variety of industrial 
production systems. It is noted that the term “complex manufacturing systems” in this study refers 
to stochastic non-serial production lines containing three particular layout situations: closed loop 
structures, parallel structures, and rework loop structures. The extended Turning Point Method 
(eTPM) will provide a systematic approach with established rule-based logic for any given 
complex lines that may have one or more of those structures. The proposed methodology has been 
implemented at an automotive assembly line with complex manufacturing layouts and evaluated 
over a period of one year production.  
The remainder of this chapter is organized as follows: Section 2.2 provides the literature review 
of definitions of throughput bottleneck, bottleneck detection methods, and the Turning Point 
Method. Section 2.3 describes the proposed methodology that extends the Turning Point Method 
from serial line to complex manufacturing systems. Section 2.4 illustrates the case study of

implementing and evaluating the proposed eTPM method in an automotive powertrain assembly 
line using real production data. The results of the proposed method are discussed. Finally, the 
conclusion and future work are given in Section 2.5.  
 
2.2 Literature Review  
2.2.1 Throughput bottleneck definitions 
There are two main types of bottlenecks in the literature. The first type is steady-state (long-
term) bottlenecks. They are usually identified at system design phase. Despite the existence of 
long-term bottlenecks, during production, several factors (system characteristics change, product 
mix, unexpected breakdowns) can cause dynamic (transient/shifting) bottlenecks to form 
(Subramaniyan et al., 2016). The successful identification of dynamic bottlenecks can support 
operational decisions.  
To date, no consensus has been reached in the definition of throughput bottlenecks in literature 
(Lawrence and Buss, 1994; Roser and Nakano, 2015). The prominent definitions used in literature 
or practices include (Kuo et al., 1996; Roser, 2002; L. Li, Chang, et al., 2009; Hopp and Spearman, 
2011; Roser and Nakano, 2015): 
(a) The bottleneck stations are those with highest long-term utilizations. 
(b) The station is the bottleneck if it has the smallest isolated production rate (PR). PR is 
defined as the average number of parts produced by a machine per cycle time.  
(c) The station is the bottleneck if the work-in-progress (WIP) inventory in the upstream 
buffer is the largest of all buffers in the systems.  

=====PAGE 27=====
 
 
14 
(d) The station is the bottleneck if it has the largest effect on slowing down or stopping the 
entire system.  
(e) Bottlenecks are processes that influence the throughput of the entire system. The larger 
the influence, the more significant is the bottleneck.  
(f) The station is the bottleneck if the sensitivity of the system production rate to a station’s 
production rate is the highest of all stations in the system, then the given station is the 
bottleneck.  
There are advantages and disadvantages in those different definitions. Definition (a) is 
quantitative and commonly accepted for evaluating the long-term system bottlenecks. In a short 
timeframe of evaluation (e.g., an hour), however, station with highest utilizations might not have 
the highest impact to the overall system throughput.  Definitions (b) and (c) are quantitative and 
can be easily calculated for theoretical analysis. Both definition (b) and definition (c) are suited 
for analyzing systems bottlenecks when large buffer capacities exist so that queues can build up. 
However, it is not suited for common manufacturing systems with limited buffer capacity. 
Definitions (d), (e), and (f) come from the perspective of TOC. The clear advantage is that they 
consider the throughput bottlenecks from a system point-of-view. They are also capable of 
understanding and defining dynamic (transient) bottlenecks in a system. Among them, definition 
(f) has an advantage of being a quantitative definition. It can be mathematically represented as 
(Chang et al., 2007):

2.2.2 Bottleneck detection methods 
In order to improve the overall throughput of the manufacturing systems, it is essential to 
identify effective methods to detect the throughput bottleneck. In the last two decades, significant 
work has been done in the area of bottleneck detection and can mainly be categorized into three 
groups: analytical methods, simulation-based methods, and data-driven methods.  
Analytical methods aim to develop closed-form mathematical models to describe the 
throughput by assuming a statistical distribution of the machine performance metrics, such as 
machine downtime, up-time, and cycle time. However, it is difficult to obtain closed-form 
expression for a serial line with more than two stations and one buffer (Dallery and Gershwin, 
1992). Approximation methods are therefore used based on either aggregation or decomposition 
approaches. Meerkov and his group studied aggregation method for Bernoulli, geometric, and 
exponential machine reliability models, and proved the convergence of their aggregation 

=====PAGE 29=====
 
 
16 
procedures (Kuo et al., 1996; Li and Meerkov, 2000; Chiang et al., 2001). Gershwin has made 
significant contributions in the decomposition method (Gershwin, 1987). The analytical models 
handle lines with various assumptions and simplifications. When production system gets complex, 
the computation would turn intractable. The statistical assumptions for station metrics limit its use 
cases to long-term steady-state (static) bottleneck detection problems (Li et al., 2009).  
Simulation-based method is the second category of work, which is effective in performing 
sensitivity-based throughput analytics. It has been implemented in industry (Chang et al., 2007) 
such as automotive assembly systems (Alden et al., 2006). In the simulation methods, discrete 
event simulation models are often created for production lines. The main advantage of the 
simulation-based throughput analysis and bottleneck detection method is that it can detect 
bottlenecks in complex production lines (Li et al., 2009). However, simulation-based models often 
require longer development time and difficult to achieve real time computation. The construction 
of the model often involves many approximations and assumptions to limit its ability to detect the 
true bottlenecks in production system (Subramaniyan, et al., 2018).  
Data-driven based model is the last category of bottleneck detection methods, which detects 
bottleneck through analyzing real-time data collected from the manufacturing systems (Li et al., 
2009). There has been an increased research effort in this area due to several distinct potential 
advantages. First, it requires less assumptions and approximations of the station performance. 
Second, real-time production data can be used to analyze true system dynamics (Subramaniyan et 
al., 2016). At last, compared to simulation-based models, data-driven approaches are usually 
flexible in applications, requiring less set-up time to create the model in a new system and capable 
of adapting to system change.

Industrial practices for detecting manufacturing system bottleneck using production data 
include evaluating queuing length, cycle time, and utilization rate. These methods are simple to 
use and easy to understand, however with respective limitations in accurately identifying 
bottleneck stations (Roser and Nakano, 2015). Well-known data-driven bottleneck detection 
approaches that are derived from the TOC perspective include: the “Arrow-based Method” (Kuo 
et al., 1996; Chiang et al., 2001), the “Active Period Method” (Roser, 2002; Roser and Nakano, 
2015; Subramaniyan et al., 2016), and the more recently “Turning Point Method” (Li, et al., 2009; 
Yu and Matta, 2016). The Arrow-based Method can be summarized as detecting bottleneck 
through comparing the operations of two adjacent stations. For example, if the blockage time of 
the upstream station is higher than the starvation time of the downstream adjacent station, then the 
bottleneck must be downstream, therefore forming an arrow pointing downstream. When arrows 
intersected, it indicates a bottleneck station has been identified. The Active Period Method defines 
that real-time bottleneck stations are those with the longest uninterrupted “active periods”, during 
which other stations are waiting for that station (Roser et al., 2002). The Turning Point Method 
works intuitively through the heuristics that bottleneck station would cause the upstream stations 
to block and downstream stations to starve. Therefore, the turning of the blockage/starvation 
behavior is a reference point for potential bottleneck stations. An overview of these well-

2.2.3 The Turning Point Method 
The Turning Point Method utilizes the manufacturing system blockage and starvation data to 
define a bottleneck. The blockage and starvation pattern captures the manufacturing system 
dynamics by reflecting the nature of workpiece flowing across the production line. Bottleneck 
stations are ones that tend to cause the upstream stations to be blocked and downstream stations to 
be starved. Besides, bottleneck stations should have higher utilizations compared to nearby 
stations, therefore with a lower total blockage plus starvation time than its adjacent stations.  
Therefore, the “turning point” can be defined at the stations where the trend of blockage and 
starvation turns from higher blockage over starvation to higher starvation over blockage. Besides, 
the “turning point” stations must also satisfy having less total blockage plus starvation time over 
its two neighboring stations. The mathematical definition is shown below:

In literature review, there is limited research that systematically addresses data-driven 
bottleneck detection for complex manufacturing layouts. The original author of the Turning Point 
Method proposed an aggregation-based solution to extend the Turning Point Method to complex 
lines (Li, 2009). The main idea is to replace each complex structure with a single equivalent station. 
The proposed approach has two main steps. The first step is to convert each complex structure in 
the line with a single pseudo station so that the complex line becomes a serial line. The author 
proposed rules for constructing the pseudo station based on production incidents. For example, a 
pseudo station is selected as the station with least starvation in a parallel structure. The second step 
is to perform bottleneck detection using the Turning Point Method on the main serial line with 
pseudo stations replacing the complex structures.  
However, this approach has three main issues. First, it simplifies the line dynamics by 
assuming that bottleneck stations in a parallel structure will have the shortest starvation time. A 
commonly seen counter-example is when a bottleneck station exists at one branch of a parallel 
line, it could cause the first stations (non-bottleneck) in the other branches to reach zero starvation 
time. This is due to the bottleneck effect of the overall parallel structure causes the buffer before 
the parallel structure to reach full capacity. In this case, the existing method would have trouble

Closed loop structures are often seen in automotive and semiconductor manufacturing 
facilities. One common type is called closed loop U-shape structure in automotive industry, 
constructed with elongated length over width, exhibiting an overall narrow and long contour. There 
are several advantages of a closed loop structure over the traditional serial production line. First, 
by reducing the distances between different stations, it allows operators to work simultaneously at 
different stations, therefore improving the overall line flexibility. For example, the operator 
standing at the first station can load and unload product units at the same time. Second, it reduces 
walking distance between stations, enhancing communication and reactivity between stations. 
Third, the pallets holding the work in progress (WIP) can conveniently recycle at the end of the 
line to enter the next cycle of production.

bottleneck rules for the closed-loop system. For example, when station 1 (as seen in Figure 2-4) is

2.4.2 Implementation 
To automate the bottleneck detection process, a cloud-based bottleneck analytics tool is built 
from scratch in Python. At the end of each shift (8 hours), the graphical incident chart generated 
by the factory information system is set up to be automatically sent to the targeted server on the 
cloud. The program is set up to run continuously in the background to check if there are new data 
arriving at the cloud-based server. When new data arrives, the program will be triggered to scan 
over the pixels at each row of the data to extract the features of instance durations. After feature 
extraction, the proposed bottleneck detection algorithm is run to detect the bottlenecks within the 
shift. 
In the pilot study, there are three types of complex structures in the production line as shown

equations (2.9) and (2.10) were adopted to consider their connection property. There are two 
parallel structures existing in the line. Stations S10a and S10b are two fully manual parallel stations 
doing assembly work. Meanwhile, station S15 is parallel with stations S16-S20. During detection, 
logic operation (2.11) was used to conjugate them into two pseudo stations so that bottleneck 
detection can be performed first in the main line. In addition, R1 and R2 are rework stations. R1, 
R2, S8, and S9 form the rework loop structure. Logic operation (2.12) was used to conjugate these 
four stations as one pseudo station. If any of the pseudo stations were detected as bottlenecks, 
propositions 2 and 3 were used to determine the bottlenecks within the subsystems.

automatically to the factory managers through email. A sample email containing the bottleneck 
detection result is shown in Figure 2-11. The visualizations contained within the email were

analysis conducted by the consultant. First, it is fully automated and greatly reduces the time from

incorporates detailed rule-based analysis for complex system structures and real production data. 
Meanwhile, manual analysis is subject to human bias and prone to input errors. Third, the proposed 
cloud-based tool is easy to integrate with the factory information systems. The visualization and 
reporting are customizable based on the needs of the users.

CHAPTER 3  Bottleneck Prediction for Complex 
Manufacturing Systems 
 
3.1 Introduction 
In recent years, researchers have started to conceptualize ideas for predicting future 
bottlenecks. The motivation is that bottleneck in modern manufacturing systems is often non-
static, but shifting between stations due to the changes in production plan, operator skills, and 
machine performance over time (Roser et al., 2002). Present mainstream data-driven bottleneck 
identification methods can only detect current bottlenecks or prior bottlenecks (Leporis and 
Kralova, 2010). However, actions involved in mitigating the bottleneck effects may take time to 
plan and execute. When an improvement action is conducted at a station, it might no longer be the 
bottleneck in the system. The prediction of future bottlenecks therefore provides a great decision 
support for factory management to foresee and formulate appropriate actions to achieve cost-
effective improvement of the system throughput.  
Bottleneck predictions are still at an early stage of research and remain a challenging task in 
literature. There are multiple factors jointly affecting bottleneck conditions, such as station 
performance, machine degradations, line structures, operator skill levels, product release plans, 
etc. (Hopp and Spearman, 2011). Existing work in literature can be summarized into two 
categories:  

=====PAGE 64=====
 
 
51 
(1) Time series prediction. Bottleneck conditions are often observed to be correlated with 
station historical performances. Statistical time series methods, such as ARMA (autoregressive-
moving-average) model, have been applied to capture such temporal trends to forecast future 
system behaviors (Li et al., 2011; Subramaniyan et al., 2018).  
(2) Event driven prediction. Bottleneck conditions can be estimated based on future prediction 
events, such as operator shifts, production mix, etc. To model such input-output relationships, 
neural network and fuzzy logic based methods have been attempted (Liu et al., 2009; Cao et al., 
2012).  
Existing research made various assumptions to simplify the modeling and lower the input 
dimension. As a result, those models cannot accurately represent the real dynamics of the 
bottleneck in manufacturing systems. For example, current time series-based methods utilized 
traditional ARMA model. It assumes a linear mapping between system observations, and does not 
incorporate additional decision inputs. Existing event driven prediction approaches do not consider 
the temporal patterns during modeling, such as operator performance shifting and machine 
degradation. A methodology combining the strengths of both time series prediction and event 
driven prediction should be explored.  
Through literature review, it is discovered that Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network is 
a good candidate for modeling bottleneck prediction problems that address the above issues. 
LSTM network is a variant of recurrent neural network that is capable of handling high-
dimensional input and learning non-linear order dependence in sequence prediction problems. In 
recent years, researchers have applied LSTM to advance the state of the art in many challenging 

=====PAGE 65=====
 
 
52 
fields, such as handwriting recognition, language modeling and translation, and traffic prediction 
(Azzouni and Pujolle, 2017).   
This research aims at designing a LSTM-based model tailored for dynamic bottleneck 
prediction problems in complex manufacturing systems with non-serial layout and producing 
multiple product variants (multi-job). Instead of predicting the bottleneck locations directly, the 
proposed methodology first predicts future blockage time and starvation time within the system. 
Station blockage and starvation times are good indicators for bottlenecks and capable of reflecting 
the future production system material flow states. LSTM is applied to individual stations to map 
both time series trends and production events in predicting future blockage and starvation times. 
Afterwards, the data-driven bottleneck detection technique, proposed in Chapter 2, is employed to 
identify the future bottleneck station in complex manufacturing systems  (Lai et al., 2021) 
The remainder of the chapter is organized as follows: Section 3.2 provides a brief literature 
review of multi-job manufacturing systems, existing bottleneck prediction methods, as well as 
Long Short-term Memory network. Section 3.3 describes the proposed methodology for 
dynamically predicting bottleneck. Section 3.4 illustrates the implementation of this method in a 
case study of a powertrain assembly line. The prediction results of the proposed method are 
discussed. Finally, the conclusion and future work are given in Section 3.5.   
3.2 Literature Review 
3.2.1 Multi-job manufacturing systems 
In modern manufacturing industry, production systems are often designed with the flexibility 
to handle multiple product variants on the same line. Such systems are especially common in the 

=====PAGE 66=====
 
 
53 
production of highly customizable commodities with diverse feature options, such as in the 
automotive assembly plants, or computer and electronics plants. The daily production plans can 
have a major impact in its production dynamics and bottleneck locations.  
A recent paper provided detailed descriptions for such modern multi-job manufacturing 
systems (Alavian et al., 2017). Some key characteristics are listed: 
• The performance of a required system is specified in terms of the product-mix, 
changing on a daily basis. 
• The jobs are released one-by-one according to the product-mix.  
• All jobs undergo identical sequence of manufacturing operations, but require different 
amount of work at some or all operations.  
• The stations are unreliable and experience random breakdowns. 
The characteristics of supporting varying product-mix gives multi-job production system some 
unique challenges in operation management. In practice, multi-job manufacturing systems usually 
experience throughput loss of up to 15% (Alavian et al., 2017). The hindrance of improving its 
productivity is due to the lack of the theoretical analysis and improvement tailored for the modern 
multi-job manufacturing systems. Despite many traditional techniques were proposed to address 
mixed-model throughput optimization (Boysen et al., 2009), such as leveling the schedule 
(Miltenburg, 1989) and minimizing work overload (Li et al., 2012), nevertheless, these approaches 
often do not consider stochastic events within the production and difficult to be integrated with 
real-time production data for dynamic or predictive decision making. Therefore, it is motivating 
to develop the bottleneck prediction tool, which adds theoretical support for modern multi-job 
manufacturing systems throughput improvement.   

=====PAGE 67=====
 
 
54 
3.2.2 Bottleneck prediction methods  
The existing work of bottleneck prediction can be summarized into two categories: time series 
prediction and event driven prediction.  
Time series prediction methods predict future bottlenecks according to the projected trend of 
historical states. It is observed that bottleneck conditions at a station are often associated with their 
past observations, due to factors such as operator performance shifting and station degradations. 
To capture such temporal dependency, researchers proposed using ARMA statistical time series 
model to predict future bottleneck indicators based on the historical observations. In 2011, Li et al. 
conducted research in this category using ARMA models (Li et al., 2011). Future blockage and 
starvation times are selected as the predicted indicators so that the future bottlenecks can be 
identified by the Turning Point Method. Another research study proposed an ARMA time series 
approach in predicting a different bottleneck indicator, active periods (Subramaniyan et al., 2018). 
Then, a well-recognized data-driven bottleneck detection method, Active Period Method, is used 
to detect future bottlenecks. The main idea of existing time series approach is to forecast bottleneck 
indicators (e.g., blockage and starvation times, active periods) in the future production period. 
Subsequently, existing data-driven bottleneck detection algorithms can be deployed to identify 
future bottlenecks. Hence, a main objective of the time series based prediction is to forecast system 
bottleneck indicators accurately, so that the bottleneck detection algorithms can provide correct 
detection of future bottlenecks. 
The second category of bottleneck prediction method is event driven prediction. Researchers 
observed that there were many input factors directly affecting the line dynamics, such as product 
mix, release sequence, and operator shift, etc. Therefore, bottlenecks can be identified based on 

=====PAGE 68=====
 
 
55 
such input-output relationship. For example, Cao et al. proposed an adaptive network-based fuzzy 
inference system to directly predict future bottleneck locations based on production inputs (Cao et 
al., 2012). However, this method does not consider system temporal information. 
From the literature review, it is identified that there exists a research gap in formulating 
bottleneck prediction problems that incorporate the strengths from both time series prediction and 
event-driven prediction. The challenge lies in the construction and formulation of the bottleneck 
prediction model with the proper selection of critical features in multi-job manufacturing systems 
as well as capable data-driven analytics tools. With many factors and events jointly affecting the 
bottleneck dynamics, the model will likely experience high dimensional input with noisy data. 
Therefore, it is desirable to select a data-driven prediction approach capable of effectively 
capturing system time series dynamics and handling high dimensional inputs. 
3.2.3 Long Short-term Memory (LSTM) neural network 
Long Short-Term Memory is a type of recurrent neural network (RNN). RNNs are great for 
modeling dynamical systems as they keep trace of previously acquired information while operating 
on input information (Hsu, 2018). Among all RNN architectures, one of the most prominent and 
accepted models is the LSTM. LSTMs overcome the optimization obstacle faced by the traditional 
recurrent neural network and have been demonstrated effective in capturing long-term temporal 
dependencies. In recent years, researchers have applied LSTM to advance the state of the art in 
many challenging fields, such as handwriting recognition, language modeling and translation, and 
traffic prediction (Azzouni and Pujolle, 2017).   
LSTM was initially introduced by Hochreiter and Schmidhuber with the objectives of 
modeling long-term dependencies and determining the optimal time lag for time series problems. 

=====PAGE 69=====
 
 
56 
The core feature of LSTM is its cell state. It acts like a conveyor belt to flow system information 
across time steps. There are four neural network structures, or called gates to carefully regulating 
the information of the cell state. These gates determine what information to forget, what 
information to add, and what information to output.  
The characteristics of LSTM are especially desirable for bottleneck prediction for the 
following reasons. First, the structure of LSTM enables the model to capture the time series 
dynamics of manufacturing systems. Second, it supports nonlinear mapping of manufacturing 
system inputs. Third, there are multiple factors affecting the bottleneck conditions. LSTM handles 
high dimensional inputs with a good tolerance of data noise (Ma et al., 2015), which is especially 
suitable for handling the data input for bottleneck prediction tasks.   
There exist several LSTM variants based on slight modifications on its structures. Greff, et al. 
did a research study comparing the popular variants of LSTM, finding that their performance is 
highly similar (Greff et al., 2015). Jozefowicz, et al. tested more than ten thousand RNN 
architectures, finding only few variations worked better than LSTMs on certain tasks, but without 
significant improvement (Jozefowicz et al., 2015).

manufacturing systems, which is shown in Figure 3-1. The proposed model combines the strengths 
of both time series prediction and event driven prediction. The model takes production system 
events into account such as future product mix and operator shift in addition to time series trend 
of system dynamics (e.g., station cycle time, blockage and starvation time) for effective bottleneck 
prediction. Moreover, LSTM neural network is employed in two stages to predict station cycle

operator dependent (if manual operations are involved). The blockage/starvation time, however, is 
subject to the network dynamics. Individual station’s cycle time in the predicted period can impact 
the blockage and starvation dynamics of the system. For example, a relatively larger average cycle 
time (compared to nearby stations) tends to increase the chance of upstream stations to be blocked 
and downstream stations to be starved.  
Therefore, a LSTM network is designed to first predict the average cycle time of individual 
stations (stage 1: station level prediction), and then use it as an input to predict blockage and 
starvation time (stage 2: system dynamics prediction).

Two variations of axle carrier products are produced daily at the line.  
All manufacturing data for this study are collected from an in-house factory information system 
(FIS) in a synchronized manner during 190 days of operations. The raw data contains two formats.

For this study, the time logs data are used, which can be processed to extract useful information

3.4.3 Results and discussions 
In this section, the bottleneck prediction results of the proposed model are presented. With the 
selection of the Turning Point Method for bottleneck detection, the objective is to predict (one day 
ahead) the future system starvation and blockage times as accurately as possible, compared with 
the true values collected in the operation data. In this study, the proposed LSTM based dynamic 
bottleneck prediction model is therefore evaluated by comparing the station level blockage and 
starvation prediction with benchmark models: (1) naïve forecast (NF), (2) ARMA model , and (3) 
neural network model.  
For the NF model, the next time step’s blockage and starvation is forecasted using the most 
recent observations. This method is used as a baseline benchmark against more sophisticated 
models (Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, 2018). For the ARMA model, this case study follows the 
implementation of the original paper (Li et al., 2011). Data preprocessing is performed by 
removing the non-stationarity of historical blockage and starvation time series data through 
differencing. Then the AIC metrics are calculated to determine the proper order for the AR and 
MA terms.  For the neural network model, we follow the method of existing event driven modeling 
techniques to directly map the same event feature input as the LSTM to the prediction output 
(blockage and starvation times). The same hyperparameter tuning process is followed as the 
LSTM-based method. Mean-Average-Error (MAE) and Root-Mean-Squared-Error (RMSE) are 
two metrics used to evaluate their one-day-ahead prediction performance. The prediction results 
for a critical station (sensitive to product mix change) and the overall line (average for all stations) 
are presented in Table 3-2. For illustration purpose, the prediction plots are generated for the target 
station in Figure 3-3.

Despite the great improvement in the prediction over existing methods, it is worth noting that 
the prediction performance is still far from being flawless. The errors during prediction can be 
seen from both Table 3-2 and Figure 3-3. For example, in Day 14, the LSTM model significantly 
over-predicted the starvation time for over 60 min. One main reason is that the task bottleneck 
prediction itself is challenging. Unexpected breakdowns can happen during the future production 
periods and cause an unexpected blockage and starvation situation. With the current model, such 
breakdowns are unpredictable. A possible solution to further improve the model is therefore to 
incorporate the information of the station health measured by sensors and predictive maintenance 
models. With fewer unexpected breakdowns during regular production, the model performance

should see an improvement. Meanwhile, it may be helpful to consider a detailed spatial modeling 
of each station. The bottleneck conditions at nearby stations are expected to impact each other. 
Such spatial relationships and interactions during bottleneck prediction can be further explored in 
future studies.  
Bottleneck prediction is still at an early phase for industrial adoptions. Some practical 
guidelines for implementing this methodology in industry are summarized here. First, the model 
requires intensive collection of the right manufacturing data (product mix, labor shift, blockage, 
starvation, cycle time, sequence, etc.) for a significant duration (in this case 190 days) for proper 
model training. For some manufacturing systems, the priority is to increase its digitization effort 
to enable their system to record such data with high accuracy. Second, in the model training stage, 
it is important to separate the model into training, development (evaluation), testing steps to avoid 
overfitting. The training data need to be sampled in continuous sequence to capture time series 
dynamics. The tuning of the parameters will likely take 1-2 days to evaluate different 
combinations. Last, as the bottleneck prediction methodology gets deployed in the factory, it is 
important to monitor model performance with true outcome through bottleneck detection. If a 
machine is predicted as a bottleneck for the next shift, one possible way to utilize the information 
is to assign operators to pay close attention to the station to ensure part supply and station health. 
The overall goal should be to avoid unexpected breakdowns for predicted bottleneck stations 
during operations.

3.5 Conclusion 
In conclusion, this study proposed a LSTM framework tailored for the multi-job manufacturing 
system dynamic bottleneck prediction problem. The proposed method improves prediction 
accuracy by considering both manufacturing system time series trend and production events. Key 
manufacturing features such as cycle time, product mix, and operator shift are selected for assisting 
blockage and starvation time prediction, which is analogous to bottleneck prediction. This AI-
based approach addresses the modeling challenges induced by historical high dimensional factory 
floor data and nonlinear order dependence in sequence prediction problems. This model is 
demonstrated with an automotive powertrain assembly line. The results show that the proposed 
method can achieve higher prediction accuracy compared with the current state-of-the-art 
approaches.

Dynamics Modeling and Control  
 
4.1 Introduction 
In modern manufacturing systems, unexpected disruptive events during production are one of 
the main contributors to schedule delays and throughput loss. Even though significant achievement 
has been made in recent years in the fields of Prognosis and Health Management (PHM) and sensor 
technologies (Qiu et al., 2021; Cohen et al., 2022; Zhao et al., 2022), stochastic disruptive events 
(e.g., machine faults, tool impairment, and part shortages) are still difficult to be eliminated in 
production floor (Zhang et al., 2021). Therefore, while more efforts should be made in better 
predicting and preventing the disruptions (Yue and Kontar, 2021), it is also essential to consider 
improving the “well-being” of a manufacturing system, so that when disruptions occur, the system 
can suffer minimal impact and recover quickly. Resilience is thus proposed to describe such “well-
being” of a system (Linkov et al., 2014). Originated from the Latin word “resillere”, meaning to 
“bounce back”, resilience today often describes the ability of a system to mitigate or absorb the 
impact of disruptions, and quickly recover to its normal operations (Henry and Ramirez-Marquez, 
2012; Gu et al., 2015). During the recent period of COVID-19 pandemic, manufacturers have 
experienced unprecedented supply disruptions and forced work stoppages (Belhadi et al., 2021), 

=====PAGE 85=====
 
 
72 
further accentuating the need and interests in modeling and improving manufacturing system 
resilience. 
The study on system resilience has attracted research attention across a diverse range of 
disciplines, especially in the past two decades (Hosseini, Barker, et al., 2016). Most literatures and 
models are clustered in the field of Psychology (Harvey and Delfabbro, 2004; Meredith et al., 
2011; Killgore et al., 2020), followed by Environmental Sciences and Ecology (Nelson et al., 2007; 
Viitanen and Kingston, 2014; Marchese et al., 2018). Despite many similarities at the concept 
level, resilience modeling for manufacturing systems can be considered as a separate field due to 
its unique nature and characteristics. The building blocks of a manufacturing system include 
workstations, buffers, and parts (workpieces) (Hopp and Spearman, 2011). The interactions of 
these building blocks exhibit spatio-temporal attributes during disruptions. Small disruptions at 
the wrong place and wrong time may lead to significant throughput loss, whereas others may be 
tolerable within a margin, and non-sensitive to the system (Zhang et al., 2021). 
There are limited studies so far in modeling manufacturing system resilience for real-time 
production control. Resilience control aims at maintaining an acceptable level of operational 
normalcy in the presence of disruptive events (Bauer et al., 2021). Most current studies focus on 
investigating the impact of external events (e.g., hurricanes, man-made disasters) to the factory’s 
supply chain networks (Hosseini, al Khaled, et al., 2016; Tucker et al., 2020; Bauer et al., 2021). 
These works provide tremendous values for evaluating and potentially improving the supply chain 
resilience. However, there is an overall lack of research investigating the intrinsic resilience for 
disruptions on the production floor, such as unexpected robotics errors and machine failures.

This chapter proposes a framework for resilience dynamics modeling and control for flow 
shops under disruptions using deep reinforcement learning (Deep RL). Reinforcement learning is 
one of the most active research areas in artificial intelligence, where agents learn to perform actions 
in an environment to maximize a reward (Francois-Lavet et al., 2018). With its rapid development, 
Deep RL has been applied and offering values in various real-world optimization problems, 
including in smart manufacturing systems (Qu et al., 2015; Oliff et al., 2020; Paraschos et al., 
2020). 
The proposed model is established by analyzing the spatio-temporal effects of disruptions on 
flow shops. Flow shops, also called as flow lines, are one of the most classic and prevalent 
manufacturing systems, where parts sequentially follow their operations (Ho and Chang, 1991). In 
modern flow shops, stations are often designed to handle multiple product variations with little to 
zero changeover time (Alavian et al., 2017). Since each product variant may require different 
handling and process time at individual stations, production sequencing (order dispatching) tasks 
are essential for optimizing system productivity under daily product mix constraint (Frazzon et al., 
2018). Current sequencing practice typically aims for improving throughput while reducing the 
lateness costs. To our best knowledge, there is a lack of research investigating dynamic line 
sequencing to achieve higher system resilience and throughput simultaneously in an environment 
under stochastic disruptions.

4.2 Literature Review  
4.2.1 Resilience modeling methods and applications  
In recent years, the significance of resilience modeling is gaining greater recognitions from a 
wide spectrum of research fields(Henry and Emmanuel Ramirez-Marquez, 2012; Hosseini, Barker, 
et al., 2016), due to its ubiquitous role in mitigating the risks associated with unpredictable or 
unpreventable disruptions of systems. Researchers and practitioners have provided several general 
definitions for resilience. Haimes describes resilience as “the ability of the system to withstand a 
major disruption within acceptable degradation parameters and to recover with an acceptable time 
and composite costs and risks” (Haimes, 2009). McDaniels et al., characterizes resilience as “the 
ability to maintain functionality and return to normality following a harmful event, ensuring that 
damage or disruption is proportionate, tolerable and affordable” (McDaniels et al., 2008). Overall, 
mainstream resilience definitions suggest the capability of a system to “absorb” and “adapt” to 
disruptive events, and “recover” from them (Hosseini, Barker, et al., 2016). In addition, the concept 
of resilience can be further defined when approached from a specific application domain. For 
example, supply chain resilience is defined by Ponomarov as the ability of “firm’s supply chain to 
prepare for unexpected events, respond to disruptions, and recover from them in a timely manner 
by maintaining continuity of operations at the desired level of connectedness and control over 
structure and function” (Ponomarov, 2012). 

=====PAGE 88=====
 
 
75 
System resilience assessment in literature can be classified into two main categories: 
qualitative or quantitative methods (Vugrin et al., 2011; Hosseini, Barker, et al., 2016). As shown 
in Figure 4-1, qualitative assessment typically applies conceptual frameworks and semi-
quantitative indexes (e.g., questionnaires with Likert scales) to obtain a general evaluation for 
system resilience level (Cutter et al., 2008; Pettit et al., 2010). Over the last few years, increased 
research efforts have been devoted to quantitative assessment (Ingrisch and Bahn, 2018). 
Resilience quantitative assessment, also known as resilience quantification, provides numerical 
measures and expressions of system resilience level. The quantitative methods in literature can be 
further characterized based on three criteria:  
 
(a) stochastic vs deterministic: probabilistic resilience models aim to determine system 
resilience level considering uncertain system behaviors, while deterministic models do 
not involve system randomness in the problem formulation. For example, Hosseini and 
Barker proposed a probabilistic resilience model using Bayesian network (Hosseini and 
Barker, 2016). They proposed a tool modeling relationships between several key 
stochastic infrastructure variables to provide robust building strategies. Deterministic 
models are also common in literature. For example, Bruneau et al. proposed a 
deterministic static measure for community resilience to earthquakes by evaluating the 
quality of degraded infrastructure against the targeted infrastructure quality during 
recovery (Bruneau et al., 2003).  
 
(b) dynamic vs static: dynamic models incorporate time-dependent factors when 
characterizing system resilience, while static models do not account for temporal 
effects. The selection between dynamic and static modeling highly depends on the 

=====PAGE 89=====
 
 
76 
system characteristics and application domains. For example, Taleb-Berrouane and 
Kahn proposed a dynamic resilience modeling approach for process systems through 
stochastic petri-nets couple (Taleb-Berrouane and Khan, 2019). Cox et al. proposed a 
static metric to calculate the resilience of London's transportation system, considering 
the maximum performance difference between disrupted and normal operations (Cox 
et al., 2011).  
 
(c) general vs structural-based: general resilience models quantify system resilience 
without considering the specific structures of systems, and therefore sometimes 
demonstrate greater applicability across domains (Hosseini, Barker, et al., 2016). 
Structural-based models consider the impact of system structure to system resilience 
level, which generally lead to more comprehensive system modeling and simulation. 
Examples of structural-based models include graph-based analysis, simulations, and 
fuzzy logic models (Kim et al., 2015; Thompson et al., 2020).

Through the literature review, there are two main research gaps identified. First, there is a lack

PL, TST, TUT) and therefore cannot be computed for real-time system monitoring and control. 
Based on the characteristics of manufacturing systems, a stochastic, dynamic and structural-based 
model can be considered in developing the resilience metric. Second, there is limited research for 
dynamic resilience control during production. To our best knowledge, Zhang et al. proposed the 
only work in this field (Zhang et al., 2021). Zhang’s proposed resilience control strategy was based 
on a reconfigurable manufacturing system, where problematic stations can be switched out during 
disruptions. Resilience control strategies for more general systems, such as flow shops and job 
shops, are not investigated in literature. 
 
4.2.2 Deep reinforcement learning in production planning and control 
Deep reinforcement learning (Deep RL) is a subfield of machine learning that integrates 
reinforcement learning with deep neural networks. Its main idea involves training an agent through 
interacting with an environment to form a policy that maximizes the return of long-term rewards 
(Francois-Lavet et al., 2018). In recent years, applying Deep RL for sequential decision-making 

=====PAGE 92=====
 
 
79 
tasks has been extensively explored, with some high-profile success in solving general Atari game-
playing (Hessel et al., 2017), routing problems (Nazari et al., 2018), and graph optimization (Dai 
et al., 2017). Despite the growing popularity and recognition, it remains nontrivial to deploy the 
algorithms directly in many real-world applications (Hester et al., 2017). One common challenge 
is setting up an accurate simulator (environment) so that the agent can learn in a proper domain 
the real consequences for its actions (Kemmerling et al., 2021).  
Recent studies investigate the applicability of Deep RL in production planning and control 
(PPC). PPC aims to fulfil the general production targets while keeping production costs low 
through strategies like advanced order release and sequencing (Bueno et al., 2020). With 
manufacturers facing more customized orders, fluctuating demands, and unpredictable supply 
disruptions, traditional PPC approaches (e.g., priority dispatching rules, multiple exact methods, 
meta-heuristics) appear inadequate in handling the increased complexity and computing close-to-
optimal solution in real time (Kuhnle et al., 2021). Early studies on applying Deep RL for PPC 
have shown competitive results. The notable advantages include the ability to continuously refine 
the solution with little human supervision, and the significant reduction in online computation time 
(Kemmerling et al., 2021). 
The integration of the state-of-the-art Deep RL algorithms to PPC modeling is demonstrated 
in multiple recent studies. Qu et al. adopted a Q-learning RL agent to control a multi-stage 
production system with three stations, with the objective of minimizing the overall wait time (Qu 
et al., 2015). A customized Python environment was created for training and evaluating the system 
to compare against a First-In-First-Out (FIFO) dispatching rule and a customized greedy heuristics. 
Waschneck et al. presented one of the first Deep RL scheduling application for a semiconductor 
wafer fabrication process (Waschneck et al., 2018). A MATLAB customized production 

=====PAGE 93=====
 
 
80 
environment was built for training three Deep Q-Network (DQN) agents, with the objective of 
minimizing cycle time spread through controlling three stations. Thomas et al. employed a single 
DQN agent for a job shop scheduling model, with the objective of maximizing throughput 
(Thomas et al., 2018). A neural network model was introduced in conjunction to monitor dynamic 
bottlenecks. Zhang et al. proposed a methodology for automatically learning priority dispatching 
rule via an end-to-end Deep RL agent during job shop scheduling (Zhang et al., 2020). The work 
innovatively integrated a graph neural network model to embed encountered states for more 
effective training on large-scale job shop systems.  
Based on the literature survey, several research gaps can be recognized. First, there is limited 
work investigating the formulation for flow shop systems. Deep RL-based dynamic production 
control is more commonly studied in job shops and batch production processes (Kemmerling et 
al., 2021). Second, few literature studies follow standard protocols in creating agent-environment 
interface. Consequently, created customized simulation environment lacks compatibility for others 
to compare and benchmark results. Third, the objectives of current studies mostly focus on 
throughput improvement and cost reduction, without considering resilience control. Resilience 
control is still at an early stage of research for manufacturing systems. 
 
4.3 Proposed Methodology  
The section explains the proposed resilience dynamics modeling and control strategy for flow 
shops under potential disruptions. The illustrated concept is shown in Figure 4-2. The proposed 
methodology aims to deploy a dispatch agent to evaluate the dynamic states of the flow line with 

=====PAGE 94=====
 
 
81 
the remaining daily order, and use its learned policy to deduce the next product variant to enter the 
line that maximizes the expected total resilience and throughput in the system.  
There are three key components in the systems. (1) Remaining Orders is the pool of all 
products remained to be released (sequenced) into the production line. (2) Flow Line consists of 
the group of connected stations and buffers in the system. (3) The Agent is a dynamic controller 
that optimizes the dispatch sequence of remaining products entering the flow line. In reinforcement 
learning, the main idea involves training an agent through interacting with an environment to form 
a policy that maximizes the expected sum of rewards. In this case, the environment includes Flow 
Line and Remaining Orders. Their dynamics states are affected by the Agent’s actions. In response, 
the environment produces respective states and reward values as feedback to the Agent. 
In addition, the framework consists of two data-driven computation modules: Bottleneck 
Detection Model and Resilience Dynamics Model. These two modules are not computed by 
artificial intelligence (machine learning). Instead, the models are established using factory physics 
and the domain knowledge of manufacturing system dynamics. Bottleneck Detection Model 
evaluates the real-time flow line dynamics and detects its bottleneck locations. It follows the 
methodology in Chapter 2 (Lai et al., 2021). The Resilience Dynamics Model computes the real-
time system resilience level to generate rewards signal to the Agent. Based on the input of current 
bottleneck locations, the module is developed by evaluating the spatio-temporal effects of 
downtime in the systems. 
In this section, we will first describe the proposed Resilience Dynamics Model in detail. Then, 
it discusses the strategy to integrate these components with Deep RL for real-time resilience 
control.

(or maintain at a high-level) system throughput and resilience (𝐷𝐹𝐿𝑅4 ) during production.

The proposed methodology is evaluated with experimental studies in a Python-based 
customized stochastic production simulation. This section first discusses in detail the creation of 
the simulation environment, as well as the network design and configurations. These settings aim 
to demonstrate the general workflow for training and deploying the proposed model in conditions 
close to practical applications. Next, it introduces two cases studies evaluating the performance of

the trained Deep RL agents in dynamic order sequencing for throughput and resilience

4.4.1 Simulation environment 
Setting up correct and realistic simulation environment is critical for the successful deployment 
of the reinforcement learning based methodology. In reinforcement learning, an agent needs to go 
through training in which it learns to improve its actions by interacting with the simulation 
environment. While the training process can be lengthy, an agent can quickly make decisions 
during field application.  
In this study, we developed a Python-based customized discrete-event production simulation 
using the SimPy library and the OpenAI Gym API. The created simulation environment provides 
a powerful tool for training and evaluating RL agents under diverse production settings.  
SimPy is an open-source process-based discrete-event simulation library in Python (Matloff, 
2008). It provides various types of shared resources to model limited capacity congestion points, 
which is suited for simulating the dynamic flow shop behaviors. In this study, machine class, buffer 
class, and workpiece class are developed based on their unique characteristics and interactions to 
each other. When simulating a production line, machine and buffer instances can be generated and 
quickly assembled to targeted layout conditions.  
Gym is an open-source interface to reinforcement learning tasks developed by OpenAI 
(Brockman et al., 2016). It provides a standardized environment for publications to compare and 
benchmark their algorithms with each other. The goal of adopting the OpenAI Gym framework is 
for the work presented here to be reproducible and comparable by other researchers.

4.4.4 Results and discussions 
The results of the simulations are shown in the following figures. Figure 4-8 shows an example 
of the performance curve of the model during training. In the first subplot, the red performance 
curve indicates the total cumulative reward in a simulation episode. Its pattern shows that the agent 
improves its performance over time. In the beginning episodes, the agent is taking random 
explorations. Since then, the reward rises significantly and continuously, until it stabilizes at a high 
level around 60 episodes. The exploration rate continuously decays and the agent makes more 
deterministic actions. Overall, it demonstrates that the agent is capable of continuously learning 
through interacting with the environment to generate more optimal and robust decisions.  
In subplot two, the blue performance curve shows the makespan for each episode during 
training. The lower makespan indicates higher throughput rate during production. During training 
process, a steady throughput improvement can be seen as the total makespan decreases. However, 
there are noticeable outliers due to stochastic station breakdowns and cycle time behaviors.   
In subplot three, it shows the average resilience level (𝐷𝐹𝐿𝑅4) during each episode in training. 
As the training progresses, the agent can dynamically respond to order and line states to propose 
sequences that raise the resilience level from around 110 seconds to 130 seconds. The 20 second 
increases show that, if an unexpected breakdown happen at the line, it is expected that the system 
can handle 20 additional seconds of downtime without affecting the total throughput based on its 
dynamic buffer and bottleneck locations. The results from these subplots show that the objectives 
of improving throughput and system resilience can be achieved simultaneously.

The proposed methodology is still at an early stage of adoption for industrial applications. Here 
are some guidelines for industrial implementation. First, prior to the implementation of the 
methodology, the manufacturer should be able to identify their real-time bottleneck locations based 
on their operation data (e.g., blockage and starvation) and send the results for dynamic resilience 
analysis. It requires a high level of digitization (digital twin) capabilities for manufacturing firms. 
Capital investment is needed to transform manufacturing system digitization capabilities first to 
harvest the benefits of data-driven dynamic operation optimizations.  Second, it is essential to 
evaluate the line specific features and characteristics to adapt the model for selected industrial

introduced to the project, there are additional indicators of system resilience that can go beyond 
the resilience metric listed in the paper due to the particular application scenario. However, the 
overall framework and methodology are applicable across different systems. Third, many 
industrial systems utilize existing commercial factory simulations that are slow or incapable of 
running reinforcement learning based simulations to train models. One solution is to first pre-train 
a model in Python as described in this paper and later fine tune the model with the factory 
simulations. The detailed discussion is provided in the paper (Kemmerling et al., 2021).  
4.5 Conclusion 
There are several distinguished contributions and advantages of this study. First, it introduces 
a useful tool for real-time system resilience quantification and improvement during production. 
There has been a lack of rigorously defined resilience metric for manufacturing systems at the 
production line level. The proposed resilience metric can also be compared between production 
lines and serve as a decision input for maintenance scheduling, production planning, line 

=====PAGE 112=====
 
 
99 
designing, etc., to ensure productivity and reduce lost associated with unexpected disruptions and 
breakdowns in the system.  
Second. it offers an example to integrate three essential components together for next-
generation factories: digital simulation (digital twin), artificial intelligence (machine learning), and 
manufacturing system domain knowledge. Implementing digital twin successfully in factories 
requires not only computers, clouds, and established sensor networks, but also strong factory 
physics knowledge and manufacturing dynamics models. Through understanding manufacturing 
system bottlenecks, buffer interactions, and the spatio-temporal disruptions effects during 
production, the right use cases (resilience control) can be determined for the simulation platforms. 
There exists popular software for engineers to build factory simulation such as MATLAB 
Simulink, Simul8, Anylogic, etc. However, current simulation tools are difficult to be integrated 
with AI components such as data processing, machine learning, and deep learning algorithms. The 
proposed customized Python-built simulation environment offers an example to integrate the 
traditional discrete event simulation through the Simpy open-source library, and the advanced 
Deep RL interactions with OpenAI Gym API. The created model demonstrates the capability of 
learning and exploring effective solutions in a customizable high-fidelity data-driven simulation 
with reinforcement learning agents 
Future work can consider extending the proposed dynamic resilience control strategy for other 
specific manufacturing systems, such as the roll-to-roll systems and job shop systems. In addition 
to dynamic job dispatching, other operation decisions can be modeled and integrated, such as 
predictive maintenance and workforce management.

CHAPTER 5 Conclusions 
 
5.1 Conclusions 
This doctoral dissertation is devoted to gaining fundamental knowledge about production 
throughput and resilience in complex manufacturing systems and to proposing practical 
methodologies and tools for improving throughput and system resilience.  
Improving system throughput and resilience effectively requires a system point-of-view. 
During the development of the dissertation, the necessities of understanding system bottlenecks 
are highlighted for their critical roles in limiting the overall system performance. The successful 
identification of system bottlenecks should not consider isolated station performances. Instead, the 
dynamic workpiece flow relationships (changing pattern of blockage and starvation trends) caused 
by the bottleneck stations are used for bottleneck identification. The system point-of-view is also 
observed during the bottleneck prediction modeling. After identifying individual station’s targeted 
cycle time based on the production plan, the model considers system-level dynamics to predict the 
future blockage and starvation time of stations. In the resilience dynamics modeling and control, 
the system point-of-view philosophy is also evident. The system resilience level is jointly affected 
by the dynamic location of bottleneck stations, the upstream and downstream relationships 
between disruptions and bottlenecks, and intermittent buffer states. Currently, many research 
efforts in literature are dedicated in constructing performance models for a single station or 

=====PAGE 114=====
 
 
101 
component in the manufacturing systems. It will thus be helpful for future work to consider 
integrating those tools for system-level analysis.  
There are two growing challenges in production performance improvement for modern 
manufacturers, as identified during the development of the dissertation. One main challenge comes 
from the ever-increasing complexity of products and production systems. The same production 
lines are deployed to handle multiple product variations. Each product variant may require unique 
handling steps at production stations and go through complex route before exiting production line. 
Traditional heuristic or empirical rules are difficult to keep up with the growing decision space for 
complex optimization problems. Another obstacle comes from the unpredictable or unpreventable 
disruptions to the system, such as abrupt disturbance of supply chain and the unplanned downtime 
of machines during production. There is a need for manufacturers to evaluate their system 
resilience and generate agile decisions in response to dynamic system disruptions.  The 
development of tools that support real-time decision-making would be preferred in industry.  
The methodologies proposed in this dissertation have been formulated considering these 
practical challenges. The bottleneck detection and prediction methods are developed to include the 
complexities of production lines and customer orders in manufacturing systems. The prediction of 
system bottlenecks based on future production orders adds theoretical supports for more dynamic 
and predictive decision makings in factory. In addition, this research proposed novel 
methodologies to quantify and improve system resilience dynamically during production, 
responding to potential system failures and disruptions.  
Despite those growing challenges, there are also rising opportunities in this era for 
manufacturers to improve their production performance and resilience.  As manufactures embrace 

=====PAGE 115=====
 
 
102 
the digital transformation in their business and operations, more data are being collected and stored.  
The recent rapid development of machine learning, artificial intelligence, and simulation software 
provides powerful tools to achieve data-driven modeling. However, these methods require proper 
adaptation in the manufacturing domain so that the tools are reliable and explainable. This 
dissertation aims to provide some examples integrating advanced data-driven tools with 
manufacturing physics knowledge to combine their strengths. In the past few decades, many 
factory physics driven models and tools were developed by manufacturing experts with rich 
domain knowledge and careful investigations for use cases. There are still great room for future 
work to consider integrating the advances of artificial intelligence with those traditional models 
and result in more efficient and resilient production.  
 
5.2 Contributions of the Dissertation 
This dissertation develops methodologies to scientifically improve the current state-of-the-art 
manufacturing system-level throughput improvement capabilities. It offers examples to integrate 
the essential components together for next-generation factories: digital simulation, artificial 
intelligence (machine learning), and manufacturing system domain knowledge. Through 
understanding manufacturing system dynamics and advanced data-driven models, several useful 
models are proposed.  
First, the dissertation develops a systematic approach for bottleneck detection for complex 
manufacturing systems with non-serial configurations. Most existing data-driven bottleneck 
detection tools focus on serial line detections. The main contribution of this study is to extend a 
well-recognized bottleneck detection algorithm, “the Turning Point Method”, to complex 

=====PAGE 116=====
 
 
103 
manufacturing systems by evaluating and proposing appropriate heuristic rules. Several common 
industrial scenarios are evaluated and addressed, including closed-loop structures, parallel line 
structures, and rework loop structures. The proposed methodology is also demonstrated with a 
one-year pilot study at an automotive powertrain assembly line, leading to a 30% gain in Overall 
Equipment Effectiveness (OEE).  In addition, this study provided practical examples of how to 
integrate the dynamic bottleneck detection tool with factory information systems to provide 
meaningful visualizations and decision input that drive continuous improvement effort in factory 
floors.  
Second, the dissertation proposes a data-driven bottleneck prediction methodology for 
complex manufacturing systems. Due to multiple intrinsic and external factors jointly affecting 
dynamic bottleneck locations, bottleneck prediction remains a challenging task in literature. 
Traditional methods focus either on time series based prediction or event driven prediction. The 
main contribution of this dissertation research is to develop a data-driven prediction model 
combining the strength of both time series based method and event driven prediction. A LSTM 
network is designed and employed to capture system time-series dynamics and handle high 
dimensional production inputs. In addition, this research evaluated and selected effective 
manufacturing features for bottleneck prediction tasks. It is demonstrated with industrial case 
studies as a useful tool for predicting future bottleneck locations given future product mix and 
historical operational data. Overall, the study provides values for improving the predictive 
analytics capabilities for modern factories to achieve the throughput improvement goal.  
Finally, the dissertation develops a resilience dynamic modeling and control strategy to 
improve production throughput and resilience at a production environment with stochastic 
disruptions. There are three main contributions. First, this research proposes a new dynamic 

=====PAGE 117=====
 
 
104 
system-level resilience metric, DFTR, for real-time system monitoring and control. It is achieved 
by evaluating the dynamic bottleneck locations, buffer states, and the spatial-temporal attributes 
of disruptions.  Second, the dissertation proposes an innovative dynamic resilience control strategy 
for flow shops systems, which has not been investigated in the literature. The real-time dispatching 
task is performed through training a Deep Q-Learning based deep reinforcement learning agent. 
Third, this dissertation discusses how to apply standard protocols in creating agent-environment 
interface for manufacturing systems, and consequently, created customized simulation 
environment that improves its compatibility for researchers to compare and benchmark results.  
 
5.3 Future Work 
There are many opportunities in the future work for researchers and practitioners to advance 
the stated work in the dissertation. A summary is listed below: 
Dynamic data-driven bottleneck detection remains an important topic and a critical decision 
input for system-level throughput analysis. Future studies can consider further expanding the 
bottleneck detection capabilities to other complex manufacturing scenarios. Bottleneck detection 
in some specific production domains still needs more careful studies, for example, battery 
production processes (continuous roll-to-roll process) and semiconducotr manufacturing processes. 
In addition, it would be valuable to investigate proper integration of the proposed bottleneck 
detection tools systematically with a manufacturing system-level digital twin for dynamic plant 
operational support.  The costs and rewards for improving bottleneck through different options 
should be jointly considered with system point-of-view, such as workforce assignment, station 
upgrades, maintenance plans.  

=====PAGE 118=====
 
 
105 
In dynamic bottleneck prediction, three future work directions are recommended. First, it is 
valuable to investigate a systematic approach to with predicted bottlenecks as decision input for 
future-oriented planning. For example, the bottleneck prediction results can potentially offer 
insights for shop floor resource prioritization to strategize workforce assignment around bottleneck 
areas. Second, it would be interesting to study bottleneck prediction for system with limited data 
(size and quality). When a new production system is in a launch stage, there are insufficient data 
to build up an accurate and robust data-driven model for bottleneck prediction. A potential solution 
is to explore the transferability of models learned from a source domain (system with abundant 
data) to a target domain (system with insufficient data).  Third, the bottleneck conditions at nearby 
stations are expected to impact each other. The current model only considers the upstream and 
downstream relationships of nearby stations. More complex spatial relationships and interactions 
during bottleneck prediction can be further explored with the recent rapid development of graph 
neural network based models. 
The dynamic modeling and control for manufacturing system resilience is still at an early stage 
of research. This dissertation only proposed dynamic resilience control for the traditional flow line 
layouts. More work is suggested to extend the method to a broader range of manufacturing 
systems, including roll-to-roll systems, job shops, and reconfigurable manufacturing systems. It is 
interesting to investigate the effective modeling strategies of system-level resilience for these 
systems and generate effective resilience control solutions. In addition, this study only considered 
line sequencing (dynamic order dispatching) as available actions for dynamic resilience and 
throughput control. There are several additional decision options that can be introduced and 
modeled, such as dynamic workforce management, proactive maintenance scheduling, and system 
reconfigurations. There are additional decision inputs that can be introduced to further improve 

=====PAGE 119=====
 
 
106 
the current model as well. For example, the remaining useful life prediction for key production 
stations can further improve the estimation of the relative risk ratio and system resilience level. 
Lastly, the proposed resilience dynamics model should be further evaluated and tested with real-
time production data and in specific industrial applications. Despite the current model is developed 
with close corporation with industrial partners, all the station variables are synthetically generated. 
As the model gets deployed in the industry, the detailed design of system agents, states, and 
rewards might be modified to better meet the practical needs in the factory floor and deliver the 
promising real-world impacts.
