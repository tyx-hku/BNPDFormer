A B S T R A C T
Bottleneck prediction is essential for improving throughput in production systems and is a key research focus. 
However, the dynamic nature of bottleneck locations, driven by complex and evolving production environments, 
presents significant challenges in this field. Existing methods often struggle to capture the intricate relationships 
among influencing factors, leading to limited interpretability and predictive accuracy. To address these issues, 
we propose an Event causal graph-based spatial-temporal neural network (ECGBSTNN) for bottleneck prediction. 
This approach represents machine operations and buffer states as event nodes, integrating expert knowledge 
to construct causal links, forming a dynamic, event-level causal graph. In addition, a weight matrix quantifies 
uncertain causal relationships, facilitating adaptive and interpretable causal weighting for bottleneck predic­
tion. By integrating a Graph Neural Network (GNN) with a Transformer-based architecture, ECGBSTNN captures 
both causal and temporal dependencies among events to predict future blockage and starvation times which are 
leveraged to identify potential bottlenecks. Experiments on a production system demonstrate that ECGBSTNN 
outperforms state-of-the-art methods, for blockage prediction, while providing enhanced interpretability and 
practical applicability. 
1 . Introduction
In the face of intensifying market competition, enhancing throughput 
is critical for manufacturing industries to bolster operational efficiency 
and competitiveness [1,2]. However, throughput improvement is hin­
dered by bottlenecks that constrain production efficiency [3]. In discrete 
manufacturing systems, a bottleneck is defined as the station that limits 
overall throughput. According to the theory of constraints, mitigating 
bottlenecks can significantly enhance system performance and increase 
throughput [4]. Consequently, researchers have focused on both his­
torical and real-time bottleneck detection to optimize manufacturing 
operations [5–7]. Although detection identifies current issues, it cannot 
forecast future conditions, making bottleneck prediction increasingly 
essential. The approach of prediction has gained prominence, enabling 
proactive operational adjustments and precise maintenance scheduling 
to improve production efficiency [8,9].
Bottleneck detection in manufacturing primarily relies on 
simulation-based and statistical analysis methods. Simulation-based 
approaches [10,11] model production processes to identify potential 
bottlenecks by simulating operational dynamics. The complexity and 
variability of real production environments often lead to significant 
deviations from actual conditions, which limits the accuracy of these 
methods in real production environments. In contrast, analytical 
methods [12–14] utilize real-time manufacturing data for statistical 
analysis to detect bottlenecks and track their historical shifts. While 
effective for identifying past and present bottlenecks, these methods

struggle to predict future bottlenecks in dynamic environments, hin­
dering proactive scheduling and maintenance planning. Therefore, 
developing predictive methods to forecast bottleneck machines and 
enable proactive task adjustments is critical for enhancing production 
throughput and operational efficiency.
However, the dynamic complexity of manufacturing systems, driven 
by factors such as machine downtime, production schedules, and op­
erator skills, generates high-dimensional data, which poses significant 
challenges for bottleneck prediction [15]. Research has progressed from 
statistical models [16] to advanced machine learning techniques, such as 
LSTM [17,18]. However, three critical research gaps remain. Processing 
high-dimensional data with both accuracy and scalability remains un­
derexplored, as current methods struggle to balance computational 
efficiency with predictive precision in real-time applications. While 
short-term temporal dynamics are effectively modeled, capturing long-
term temporal dependencies, which are crucial for predicting delayed 
bottleneck shifts [19], is inadequately addressed. This limitation re­
stricts foresight in dynamic systems. Spatial-temporal neural networks 
capture station-level correlations [15,20,21], but feature-level relation­
ships, such as interactions between specific variables like downtime 
and queue length across stations [22], are rarely studied. Feature-level 
relationships refer to interactions between individual variables (e.g., 
upstream downtime affecting downstream queue length), in contrast 
to station-level correlations that focus on broader inter-station dynam­
ics. Addressing these challenges, including scalable high-dimensional 
data processing, robust long-term temporal modeling, and comprehen­
sive feature-level relationship mapping, is essential for enhancing the 
accuracy and applicability of bottleneck predictions in manufacturing 
systems.
To address these gaps, developing a comprehensive understanding 
of manufacturing processes is essential, systematically modeling causal, 
temporal, and dependency relationships among features to capture 
time-evolving dynamics [23]. In this context, events serve as effective 
abstractions for system changes, enabling interpretable feature-level in­
teractions and temporally informed bottleneck prediction. Events, such 
as machine failures or schedule adjustments, depend on variables like 
downtime duration or working time, making them a robust choice for 
modeling feature-level relationships and their temporal dependencies in 
dynamic manufacturing systems.
Events abstract feature interactions and temporal dynamics in man­
ufacturing systems. Analyzing their behavior reveals causal and se­
quential relationships, supporting accurate bottleneck prediction and 
system optimization. In manufacturing systems, events such as process 
initiation, completion, material flow, and machine state changes are 
recorded in log data, providing fine-grained insights into system dy­
namics and facilitating bottleneck prediction [24]. These events exhibit 
inherent causal and temporal sequential properties, reflecting the log­
ical structure and time-evolving dynamics of production systems. For 
instance, a delay in material delivery from an upstream station can trig­
ger starvation at a downstream station, illustrating causal dependencies 
that influence bottleneck formation. In complex discrete manufactur­
ing systems, capturing these spatial and contextual relationships among 
events is critical for improving the accuracy and robustness of bot­
tleneck predictions. However, the high dynamism and uncertainty of 
events, driven by factors such as variable production schedules, machine 
downtime, and operator performance [15], pose significant challenges 
for predictive models. Moreover, sequential causal chains formed by 
event interactions often exhibit delayed effects, where historical events 
continuously shape subsequent system states, complicating long-term 
dependency modeling. Consequently, advanced methods that integrate 
spatial-temporal causal relationship modeling, such as graph neural net­
works (GNNs) for spatial interactions [20] and modified Transformers 
for temporal dynamics [25], are essential to accurately predict and man­
age the evolving dynamics of production systems, enabling proactive 
maintenance and operational optimization.
Motivated by the above identified challenges and analysis, we pro­
pose a Spatial-Temporal Neural Network with Event Causal Graphs 
(ECGBSTNN) for bottleneck prediction in complex systems. To effec­
tively capture causal and temporal dependencies among event features, 
we integrate Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) to model causal rela­
tionships and Transformer-based methods to capture temporal trends. 
Events are represented as nodes in a dynamic, weighted, directed graph, 
with edges constructed based on dynamic causal interactions. These 
interactions account for uncertainties in event responses, and follow­
ing [26], a causal weight matrix is utilized to encode dependencies 
and their associated uncertainties. The event graph is processed using 
an attention-based GNN, which is combined with a Transformer-based 
temporal model to jointly capture dynamic causal relationships and 
long-term temporal patterns. This approach enables accurate predic­
tion of station blockage and starvation, which are critical indicators 
for bottleneck identification, as established in [16]. Finally, we employ 
the turning point method [27], a robust data-driven technique, to iden­
tify future bottlenecks based on the predicted blockage and starvation 
patterns.
In summary, the main contributions of our work are as follows:
1. To the best of our knowledge, the ECGBSTNN framework is 
the first to leverage causal event relationships and their tempo­
ral dynamics for interpretable bottleneck prediction in complex 
manufacturing systems.
2. Events are used to represent feature-level interactions and are 
modeled as graph nodes, with edges capturing their causal and 
sequential dependencies. This reveals system dynamics and im­
proves interpretability and prediction accuracy.
3. The proposed method uses Graph Neural Networks to model dy­
namic causal event relationships and a Transformer with a causal 
mask to capture long-term temporal dynamics in a production 
system.
The paper is structured as follows: Section 2 reviews bottleneck pre­
diction and graph neural network advancements. Section 3 describes the 
proposed spatio-temporal neural network. Section 4 presents a produc­
tion line case study and benchmark comparisons. Section 5 concludes 
the paper.
2 . Related work
The section provides a structured overview of the research back­
ground and recent developments. Section 2.1 reviews the evolution from 
bottleneck detection to prediction, highlighting the limitations of exist­
ing temporal and spatiotemporal methods, and motivating event-level 
causal modeling with GNNs and Transformers to learn event causality 
and long-term dependencies. Section 2.2 focuses on event-driven ap­
proaches, discussing causal graph learning and long-term dependency 
modeling, and emphasizing the effectiveness of adaptive adjacency 
matrices and attention mechanisms.
2.1 . Bottleneck detection and prediction
In manufacturing systems, bottlenecks refer to machines that limit 
overall throughput [28]. Bottlenecks are affected by multiple factors, 
and various methods have been applied to mitigate them and en­
hance throughput. Analytical approaches [13,29] rely on theoretical 
equations, while simulation-based methods [10,11] use discrete event 
simulations to identify bottlenecks. However, both face challenges in 
dynamic environments due to their limitations in adapting to chang­
ing conditions. Data-driven approaches [12,14] are better suited for 
dynamic production environments, leveraging real-time historical pro­
duction data to analyze bottlenecks. Over the past decade, data-driven 
methods have demonstrated significant efficacy in both academic and 
industrial settings by leveraging real-time production data to detect

and mitigate bottlenecks, outperforming conventional analytical and 
simulation-based approaches. However, these methods are largely retro­
spective, providing limited predictive capability for system states, thus 
constraining operators’ ability to plan and optimize throughput. As a 
result, research has increasingly shifted toward developing bottleneck 
prediction techniques.
Many efforts aim to provide predictive insights for proactive sys­
tem optimization. Most existing approaches directly input raw data into 
predictive models, which autonomously learn data relationships and 
input–output mappings. Traditional methods model these relationships 
by extracting rules from the data. The ARMA-based methods [30,31] 
leverage linear combinations to model time series, suitable for simple, 
stationary data. In contrast, ANFIS [17] utilizes fuzzy rules and neu­
ral networks to capture nonlinear input–output mappings, effectively 
handling complex production data. However, these methods face chal­
lenges with nonlinear dynamics and demand intensive computational 
resources for high-dimensional features. To address the limitations of 
traditional methods, researchers have increasingly employed machine 
learning techniques to model data relationships and capture temporal 
dependencies in time series, based on their robust feature extraction 
and generalization abilities. Lai et al. [32] employ a Long Short-Term 
Memory (LSTM) network to process high-dimensional data for predict­
ing future bottlenecks, integrating historical data with future production 
planning inputs. Fang et al. [19] proposed a parallel gated recurrent 
units (P-GRUs) network to extract the temporal correlations from main 
inputs and auxiliary inputs for bottleneck prediction. Liu et al. [18] 
verified the roles of different features in the prediction using a Long 
Short-Term Memory network with a dual attention mechanism (LSTM-
DAM). Although the above have employed machine learning to predict 
time-evolving production bottlenecks by capturing temporal correla­
tions in high-dimensional historical data, they often overlook the feature 
relationships associated with production line layout and material flow.
Researchers have found that production data not only exhibit dy­
namic temporal dependencies but also, with deeper investigation, re­
veal inherent spatial features determined by manufacturing processes. 
Consequently, related research methods have gradually evolved from 
temporal prediction approaches to spatial-temporal models that inte­
grate both temporal dependencies and spatial relationships. They predict 
the bottleneck indicators in the future period and leverage them to 
identify the future bottlenecks in a manufacturing system. Ma et al. 
[22] highlighted that production data exhibit temporal and spatial rela­
tionships across stations, with shifting bottlenecks influenced by these 
temporal-spatial features. Consequently, they proposed the TUSB-LSTM 
(integrated LSTM with Bi-LSTM) method to capture temporal relation­
ships within individual station features and spatial correlations among 
stations. However, the spatial correlations captured by these models may 
lack accuracy. Lai et al. [15] mapped an assembly line layout into a 
graph based on the material flow and proposed a BSTAN (integrating the 
graph attention neural network, GAT and gated recurrent unit, GRU) to 
extract the spatial dependencies among stations using GAT and capture 
the time-evolving dynamics of features. Besides, Yin et al. [20] noted 
that a change in one task can trigger changes in others and proposed 
a spatio-temporal graph convolutional network (BTGCN) to model the 
cascading effects and temporal dependencies in the data. Moreover, Wu 
et al. [21] argued that the primary challenge in bottleneck prediction 
stems from effectively utilizing knowledge of sustainable production 
processes. They proposed the BPSTK method, which constructs a knowl­
edge graph of production processes and resources, integrating GAT with 
GRU to predict bottlenecks. Moreover, our last work divided the features 
into temporal and spatial features and employed a parallel model (Dual-
BDSTN [33]) to learn the temporal and spatial relationships. Although 
graph-based methods can extract spatial correlations by mapping sites 
or tasks to graph nodes, each node often encompasses multiple features, 
which may limit their ability to accurately capture the spatial relation­
ships among these features. Therefore, it is essential to treat features as 
graph nodes and model their transitive relationships to capture feature 
interactions effectively.
Above all, existing bottleneck prediction methods have evolved from 
purely temporal models to spatio-temporal frameworks that incorporate 
structural information. However, most approaches focus on station-
level representations, where multiple features are aggregated within 
each node, limiting their ability to capture fine-grained feature interac­
tions. In addition, these methods often lack explicit modeling of causal 
relationships and struggle to capture long-term temporal dependen­
cies simultaneously. These limitations motivate the need for a more 
fine-grained, event-driven, and causality-aware modeling framework.
While modeling feature dependencies is essential, understanding the 
event-level interactions that drive these dynamics is equally critical 
for accurate bottleneck prediction. Previous research on the interactive 
dynamics between production events remains limited. The production 
rhythm of the system is captured through recorded events in log data, 
which provide finer granularity to accurately reflect the system’s op­
erational status [34]. In discrete manufacturing systems, events are 
independent and have sequential relations (causality effects) [23]. To 
accurately capture these effects, methodologies must integrate causal 
information inherent to the production system. The causal relation­
ships among events are complex and non-trivial, influenced by diverse 
tasks and external disruptions that affect product flow. Moreover, vari­
ations in event response times induce temporal dependencies, reflecting 
the sequential dynamics inherent in the system. On the other hand, 
to effectively model long-term feature dependencies, the Transformer 
model [25] employs multi-head attention mechanisms, achieving supe­
rior performance in capturing temporal dynamics. Therefore, we can 
develop mappings of events into causal graphs by integrating expert 
system knowledge and employing graph neural networks to model dy­
namic event relationships, while designing modified Transformers to 
capture both long-term and short-term temporal dynamics for bottleneck 
prediction.
2.2 . Capturing causal and temporal events using spatio-temporal neural 
networks
Event-driven approaches enhance the responsiveness and adaptabil­
ity of equipment monitoring in manufacturing systems. Processing tasks 
are closely related to system states and can be captured through mon­
itored events [35]. There exists a cause-effect associative evolutionary 
mechanism among them [36]. Modeling causal relationships between 
events as a knowledge graph aids in revealing underlying system mech­
anisms and has demonstrated effectiveness in applications like fault 
diagnosis, clinical medicine, and traffic networks. In manufacturing sys­
tems, event occurrences exhibit causal relationships similar to those 
observed in fault diagnosis and traffic networks. Therefore, events in 
manufacturing processes can also be mapped to nodes and their interac­
tions represented as edges in a graph structure. Many approaches employ 
spatial-temporal neural networks to capture their patterns. They de­
scribe event relationships through dynamic semantic information[34]. 
While many leverage data-driven methods to assign the nodes and edges 
with weights, such as equal weights, rule-based weights [15,37] and 
learned weights [38,39].
In spatial-temporal networks, traditional GNNs with fixed equal 
edge weights inadequately capture dynamic event causality. Learnable 
edge weights are thus introduced to adaptively model time-varying 
causal dependencies, enhancing spatial-temporal representations. Graph 
Attention Network (GAT) introduces an attention mechanism that dy­
namically captures the importance of relationships between nodes. Such 
bidirectional weighting struggles to reflect the one-way nature of causal 
effects accurately. GCN weights are determined by calculating the spatial 
similarity of input features to integrate the graph and capture real-time 
changes in traffic states [39]. Causation entropy is applied to compute a 
weighted matrix for the GCN, enabling effective fusion of graph features

modeling long-term event feature sequences.

namically learnable edge weights and integrate attention mechanisms to 
jointly model causal relationships and long-term temporal dependencies 
between events, enabling causal event-driven bottleneck prediction.

3.3 . Event causal graph establishment
The bottleneck shifts of a production process are affected by the in­
tegration of starvation and blockage time. Accurate prediction of future 
bottlenecks requires forecasting the two states. Since they are affected 
by both station and buffer events (event features as section 3.1), an event 
causality graph is introduced to capture their causal relationships and 
dynamic interactions.
As shown in Eqs. (1) and (2), the events of a station are mutually 
exclusive in a period. Similarly, the states of a buffer are also mutually 
exclusive. Therefore, the interval events in a station or buffer have no 
causality relationships. As mentioned by [16], if the parts are not de­
livered to the downstream station or buffer in time, it may cause the 
downstream station to be starved (or the buffer to be empty), or the up­
stream station to be blocked (or the buffer to be full). This illustrates the 
causal relationships between station events and buffer events. According 
to expert knowledge, the down, operating, and starvation events of a 
station may lead to starvation in the downstream station (or an empty 
buffer). Similarly, the down, operating, and blocked events may cause 
blockage in the upstream station (or a full buffer). Based on these causal 
relationships, a event causality graph (ECA) is constructed, shown in 
Fig. 2. The ECA can be denoted as G, as described in the Section 3.1.

3.4 . Network model
This section introduces the bottleneck architecture of the proposed 
ECGBSTNN model, with the framework depicted in Fig. 3. In the in­
put layer, the event features are first transformed into an event causal 
graph with a dynamic weight matrix, as described in Section 3.3. High-
dimensional spatial features are extracted as the dataset passes through 
the Graph Attention Layer. Following positional embedding, long-term 
dependencies are captured by multi-head attention while short-term de­
pendencies are extracted using the graph attention mechanism in the 
spatial-temporal module. Finally, bottleneck indicators are predicted 
by integrating spatial and temporal dependencies under constrained 
optimization.

4.2 . Experiment results and discussions
4.2.1 . Experiment results
In the study, the TPM method is adopted to detect bottlenecks within 
the production line. The blockage and starvation time, as its core in­
dicators, are forecasted to support proactive identification of future 
bottlenecks. To evaluate the prediction performance of the proposed 
method, assessments are conducted on M1, M6, and the overall pro­
duction line. Since M1 is located at the input end of the production 
line, it is less likely to experience starvation but may frequently en­
counter blockage due to disturbances in downstream processing. This 
makes it a suitable candidate for observing blockage patterns and assess­
ing the causal modeling capability. In contrast, M6 is situated between 
M5 and M7 without an intermediate buffer, making it highly susceptible 
to becoming a bottleneck. Therefore, M1 is selected as a representative 
station for evaluating blockage prediction performance, while M6 serves 
to assess the model’s ability to capture bottleneck tendencies. M1 rarely 
experiences starvation as there are no upstream buffers or machines that 
can cause supply interruptions. Since the ground truth for M1 starvation 
is nearly zero, most models naturally yield a zero prediction error as 
shown in the results of M1 in the Table 4.

[15], BTGCN [20], and BPSTK [21], are employed as benchmarks for 
predicting blockage and starvation time. The prediction performance 
is assessed using two metrics (MAE and RMSE), evaluated on stations

structured semantic guidance. In contrast, BSTAN relies on the model

data preprocessing can enhance model performance. In addition, the 
Transformer outperforms the LSTM, indicating that the multi-head at­
tention mechanism effectively captures contextual dependencies and 
long-term temporal relationships among workshop events, especially 
those caused by data latency, thereby enhancing model performance. 
The GAT-based BSTAN and BPSTK methods achieve lower MAE than the 
GCN-based BTGCN method, indicating that the models yield relatively 
small average prediction errors and stable performance. Based on GAT 
learning spatial relations, data feature relationship preprocessing and 
the multi-head attention mechanism to improve model capabilities, the

blockage and starvation times, especially achieving reductions of 2.73 
and 2.93 in MAE and RMSE of the overall production line, respectively,

baseline model (Table 4).

for M1 and M6 are plotted in Figs. 7 and 8, respectively. In a normal 
case, M1 is located at the beginning of the production line and rarely 
experiences starvation due to sufficient incoming material. However, it 
may be blocked by downstream disturbances. As a result, the starvation 
prediction curve mostly remains at zero, showing high prediction accu­
racy. This indicates that the starvation time prediction of M1 is primarily 
driven by its temporal dynamics, with minimal influence from other 
station events, as reflected in the causal event graph modeling shown 
in Fig. 2. The blockage time of M1 and M6, and the starvation time of 
M6, are influenced by upstream and downstream events, reflecting clear 
causal and spatial-temporal dependencies. The proposed method is ca­
pable of effectively capturing these interdependent patterns with the 
causal event graph modeling. As a result, it demonstrates superior per­
formance in predicting the evolving states of blockage and starvation, as 
shown in Figs. 7 and 8. However, at 50min, the predicted value is much 
lower than the true value. It may be interrupted by the outside environ­

4.2.2 . Discussions
The experimental results show that the proposed ECGBSTNN frame­
work achieves better predictive performance than existing leading meth­
ods, as reflected by lower MAE and RMSE values. This improvement 
comes from preprocessing event feature relationships to make the model 
more interpretable, using a directed and weighted causal graph to cap­
ture dependencies between events, and combining multi-head attention 
with Graph Attention Networks (GAT) to model both long-term and 
short-term temporal patterns. Together, these elements allow the frame­
work to represent the changing dynamics of manufacturing processes 
with high accuracy.
In real production settings, each operational unit (e.g., stations, 
buffers, operators) can be represented as event states reflecting its work­
ing condition. These events exhibit natural causal links that encode the 
underlying process logic. By comparing the process graph constructed at 
the station level with the event causal graph built at the feature level, it 
is observed that in the station graph, edges between nodes influence 
each other at every time step. This may result from certain features 
of one station being related to features of other stations, though such 
relationships are not explicitly apparent in the graph structure. In con­
trast, the event causal graph, based on event features, captures direct 
causal effects between events and reveals dynamic associations among 
event features. These dynamic changes can be clearly seen through the 
attention scores in the graph. By directly modeling these causal links, 
ECGBSTNN enhances the interpretability of system behavior and pro­
vides valuable insights for data-driven bottleneck prediction. Due to the 
persistence of events, delayed effects occur, causing events to exhibit not 
only short-term but also long-term temporal dependencies. Therefore, 
we incorporate a Transformer module to capture long-term temporal 
dependencies and compare it with traditional RNN models such as 
LSTM, achieving superior results. Additionally, we design a parallel 
long and short-term temporal dependency module, which further im­
proves the model’s performance. This capability is particularly beneficial 
for production planning and maintenance, where accurate forecasting 
of bottlenecks and inefficiencies can lead to increased efficiency and 
reduced downtime.
Regarding the reproducibility of the proposed algorithm, the event 
causal graph is first constructed based on the production line process 
flow, as shown in Fig. 2. Then, using the event sequences from the 
log data (Table 1) and applying Eq. (4), the graph edges are dynami­
cally updated to capture evolving relationships. Event durations within 
each cycle are also recorded, illustrated in Fig. 5, resulting in the final 
feature-level event causal graph. With algorithm parameters set as in 
Table 2, Algorithm 2 computes the future starvation and blockage dis­
tributions, which are subsequently used by the TPM method to identify 
potential bottlenecks. This stepwise procedure provides a clear pathway 
to reproduce the algorithm. However, due to data confidentiality agree­
ments with industry partners, direct access to the original datasets is 
restricted, limiting the exact replication of the experimental results by 
external researchers.
While the current study applies this approach to a linear production 
line, the underlying event causal graph framework is highly adaptable 
and can be extended to more complex nonlinear discrete manufactur­
ing systems, where processes may run in parallel, loop back, or branch 
out. By enriching node and edge definitions, the framework can capture 
intricate relationships and feedback loops, enabling effective modeling 
of complex causal dynamics in real-world manufacturing environments. 
However, as the complexity and scale of these systems increase, the 
graph structures grow larger and more computationally demanding. 
To address these scalability challenges, techniques such as graph parti­
tioning, hierarchical graph construction, pruning, distillation and mini-
batch sampling can be applied to reduce computational overhead while 
preserving critical causal information. The incorporation of sparse atten­
tion mechanisms further enhances efficiency by concentrating computa­
tional resources on the most relevant node interactions. Additionally, 
parallel and distributed computing frameworks can be leveraged to 
accelerate training and inference. More efficient algorithms for atten­
tion computation and dynamic edge updates can also be integrated to 
optimize performance on large-scale graphs. Collectively, these advance­
ments facilitate the practical application of the proposed method across 
diverse industrial settings with complex topologies and extensive event 
interactions, while maintaining computational efficiency and predictive 
accuracy.
Although modeling workshop production states and their dynamic 
interrelations through event-based representations significantly im­
proves the predictive accuracy of future system states, several limitations 
affect the model’s extensibility and generalization. A primary limita­
tion is the coarse granularity of event classification, which may fail 
to capture subtle but important variations within production processes. 
For example, downtime events are often treated as a single broad cat­
egory, despite differing underlying causes that can result in varying 
durations and impacts. This coarse attribution restricts the model’s abil­
ity to differentiate among diverse factors within similar event types, 
reducing the precision of causal inference and long-term predictions. 
Another significant challenge stems from the sparsity of event occur­
rences. Infrequent but critical events may not be adequately recog­
nized or learned by the model due to limited data availability. This 
scarcity constrains the model’s sensitivity to rare but impactful produc­
tion states, affecting its robustness in real-world scenarios. Moreover, 
the framework currently does not fully address the heterogeneity of 
event types, such as variable product processing times, machine-specific 
downtime reasons, or operator-related disruptions, all of which can 
substantially influence system dynamics. Despite enhancements in tem­
poral modeling through Transformer modules and parallel long-short 
term dependency mechanisms, the fixed nature of these components 
may limit adaptability in highly variable or evolving production en­
vironments. Furthermore, while the model employs graph attention 
networks (GAT) to learn causal weights adaptively, these weights can 
alternatively be established via more precise, domain-informed model­
ing approaches. Such strategies can accelerate convergence and improve 
fitting efficiency, thus supporting scalability. To address these limita­
tions and enhance extensibility, future work will emphasize refining 
event categorization by incorporating more detailed and distinct event 
types, including differentiated product processing phases and specific

downtime classifications. This increased granularity is expected to better 
capture event-specific temporal patterns and causal influences, thereby 
improving generalization across varied manufacturing contexts and 
enhancing resilience against data quality issues and event sparsity. 
Additionally, exploring adaptive architectures capable of dynamically

changes will further strengthen prediction accuracy and practical ap­

matrix A can evolve from a coarse-grained representation to a more 
structured form through several methodological enhancements. First, 
node refinement allows each downtime event to be decomposed into 
subtype-specific nodes, such as failure-induced or maintenance-induced 
categories, leading to a more expressive event graph. Second, type-aware 
causal modeling ensures that entries in A are conditioned on both tem­

strengths. Additionally, regularization techniques like parameter shar­
ing can be introduced to mitigate data sparsity for rare events, while 
a dynamic updating mechanism allows A to adapt to changing produc­
tion conditions as a time-varying matrix. Automated or semi-supervised 
methods for event type adaptation and scalable graph construction will 
also be investigated to facilitate deployment across diverse industrial
scenarios.
5 . Conclusion
In this work, we propose the Spatial-Temporal Neural Network 
with Event Causal Graphs (ECGBSTNN) for predicting bottlenecks in 
complex manufacturing systems. Our method integrates Graph Neural 
Networks to capture dynamic causal relationships between events with 
Transformer-based techniques employing a causal mask to model long-
term temporal dependencies. By representing events as nodes in a 
dynamic, weighted, directed graph where edges denote uncertain causal 
interactions, ECGBSTNN effectively leverages both causal and temporal 
event information. Experimental results demonstrate that this approach 
delivers accurate and interpretable bottleneck predictions, with causal 
graphs clarifying inter-event dependencies to enhance both prediction 
performance and explainability.
From an academic perspective, this study advances spatial-temporal 
modeling by embedding causal inference within neural network ar­
chitectures and addressing the critical challenge of interpretability in 
deep learning for complex systems. The integration of dynamic event 
causal graphs with GNN and Transformer frameworks not only improves 
prediction accuracy but also enhances model transparency, paving the 
way for future research on explainable, causally grounded machine 
learning models applicable across diverse dynamic processes. From an 
industrial standpoint, ECGBSTNN addresses a fundamental challenge 
in modern manufacturing by accurately predicting bottlenecks within 
highly dynamic and interconnected production systems. By providing 
interpretable insights into the causal mechanisms underlying system 
inefficiencies, our approach empowers practitioners to optimize work­
flows, reduce costly downtime, and allocate resources more effectively. 
This contributes to smarter, data-driven manufacturing practices that 
enhance productivity and competitiveness in rapidly evolving industrial 
environments.
Based on these advances, we further present a streamlined frame­
work for industrial bottleneck prediction leveraging event evolution 
analysis. This framework offers practical guidance for optimizing op­
erations and maintenance, enabling operators to make informed, data-
driven decisions by proactively identifying bottlenecks, improving 
operational efficiency, minimizing downtime, and supporting robust 
decision-making in complex production systems.
